Do You Need a K-ETA for South Korea? (2026 Update)

By Casey, Gently Yonder editor

K-ETA for South Korea in 2026: 22 countries (US, UK, Canada, Australia, Japan and more) are temporarily exempt through 31 December 2026 — but you still need an arrival card. What changed, and how to prepare.

Updated 2026-09-09 · 5 min read

Build My Trip Checklist All guides

Short answer, current for 2026: most short-stay tourists do not need a K-ETA to visit South Korea right now. Under Korea’s “Visit Korea Year” initiative, 22 countries and regions — including the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia, Japan, and Germany — are temporarily exempt from the K-ETA through 31 December 2026. There is one catch worth knowing before you fly: if you skip the K-ETA, you’ll need to complete an arrival card on entry instead. A K-ETA is not a visa, and neither it nor this guide guarantees admission — the immigration officer at the border always makes the final call.

This is exactly the kind of rule that changes quietly and catches people out, so it’s worth two minutes now.

What is a K-ETA?

The K-ETA (Korea Electronic Travel Authorization) is an online pre-travel screening that visa-free visitors normally complete before boarding a flight to South Korea. You apply on the official portal, pay a small fee, and — if approved — it’s linked to your passport for multiple entries over its validity period. It is not a visa; it’s the layer that sits in front of visa-free entry, similar in spirit to the US ESTA or the UK ETA.

Do I need a K-ETA in 2026?

For most leisure travelers from the major visa-free markets, no — not right now. To boost inbound tourism, Korea’s Ministry of Justice has repeatedly extended a temporary K-ETA waiver for a list of 22 countries and regions, and the current extension runs through 31 December 2026. Nationals of those places can enter for short tourism stays without applying for (or paying for) a K-ETA.

The exempt list includes major markets such as the United States (including Guam), Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia, Japan, and Germany, among others. Because the list and its expiry date are set by policy and have moved before, always confirm your own nationality’s current status on the official K-ETA portal (k-eta.go.kr) shortly before you travel — that’s the authoritative source, not any blog (including this one).

The catch: you still need an arrival card

Here’s the part that surprises people. Skipping the K-ETA does not mean skipping paperwork. If you enter without a K-ETA, you’re expected to complete an arrival card — increasingly the digital e-Arrival Card, filed online, though paper forms are still handed out on many flights. Travelers who do hold a valid K-ETA are exempted from the arrival card. So the practical choice is:

  • No K-ETA (using the exemption): free, nothing to apply for in advance — but complete the arrival card on arrival.
  • Voluntary K-ETA: a small fee and a pre-trip application — but it covers you across multiple visits and skips the arrival-card step at the border.

Either path is valid. Neither one guarantees entry; both simply keep your paperwork in order.

Should I get a K-ETA anyway?

If you’re from an exempt country, it’s optional. Some travelers apply anyway because it removes the arrival-card step and stays valid for future trips within its validity window — handy if you visit Korea often. Others simply use the exemption and fill in the arrival card. If you are going to apply, use only the official portal and be wary of look-alike sites that charge inflated “service” fees for the same government form.

What happens after 2026?

The exemption is temporary. As it stands, it is scheduled to expire on 31 December 2026, after which the K-ETA could return for these nationalities unless the government extends the waiver again — which it has done several times before. If your trip lands near the turn of the year, this is the single detail to re-check on the official portal before you book flights, because “do I need a K-ETA?” may have a different answer in January than in December.

How to prepare, briefly

  • Confirm your status on k-eta.go.kr for your nationality and travel dates.
  • Have a passport with sensible remaining validity and an onward/return plan — standard for visa-free entry anywhere.
  • Decide K-ETA vs arrival card before you fly, so there’s no scramble at Incheon or Gimpo.
  • Sort connectivity so you can pull up confirmations on landing — see our guide to staying connected in South Korea.

Frequently asked questions

Is the K-ETA a visa? No. It’s a pre-travel authorization for visa-free visitors, not a visa. If your nationality needs an actual visa for Korea, the K-ETA rules don’t apply to you.

Is South Korea visa-free for US, UK, Canadian, and Australian tourists in 2026? For short tourism stays, yes — and they’re also currently in the temporary K-ETA-exempt group through 31 December 2026, so they need neither a visa nor a K-ETA at the moment (just the arrival card).

What is the e-Arrival Card? It’s Korea’s digital version of the paper arrival/entry card, which you can complete online before or on arrival. Travelers entering without a K-ETA are the ones who need it.

Does a K-ETA guarantee I’ll be let in? No. Like an ESTA or ETA elsewhere, it authorizes you to travel to the border; the immigration officer makes the final admission decision.

Where is the only official place to apply? The government portal at k-eta.go.kr. Avoid third-party sites that add fees.
