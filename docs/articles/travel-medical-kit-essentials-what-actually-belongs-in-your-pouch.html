Travel Medical Kit Essentials: What Actually Belongs in Your Pouch

By Martin, Gently Yonder writer

What to put in a travel medical kit, quantities for 1-2 weeks, carry-on vs checked liquids rules, prescription paperwork and why Japan restricts some medicines.

A good travel medical kit is small, boring and built around you: a few pain, stomach, allergy and skin items, your own regular medicines in their original packaging, and a folded page of paperwork. The UK’s national travel health service says every traveller should carry a first aid kit, and that what goes in it should be tailored to the person, the destination and the travel plans. Most of it can go in a pouch the size of a paperback. The parts that need real thought are not the plasters. They are the liquids rules at security, and the fact that some everyday medicines are restricted or banned in other countries.

This guide goes through what to pack by category, how much to take for a one or two week trip, what goes in your cabin bag and what can go in the hold, how to carry prescription medicines, and what is better bought when you arrive. It is general travel-preparation guidance, not medical advice. If a medicine, a condition or a destination makes the answer unclear, ask a pharmacist or doctor.

What to pack, by category

The categories below follow the contents list published by NaTHNaC (the National Travel Health Network and Centre, which runs the UK’s TravelHealthPro service) and the CDC’s travel health kit page. The quantities are our own rule of thumb for a 1-2 week trip, not an official figure. Follow the dosage on the packet or your pharmacist’s advice, and do not share medicines with other people.

Antibiotics, antimalarials and similar prescription-only medicines belong in the kit only if a healthcare professional has prescribed them for your trip (the CDC says the same). A kit is for minor problems and for buying time until you can see a professional; it is not a substitute for care.

A few items make the kit easier to carry. A weekly pill organiser suits loose tablets on the plane, provided you also carry the original packaging for anything prescribed. Blister plasters are the one item people most often wish they had on a walking-heavy trip, and they weigh almost nothing.

Carry-on or checked: what goes where

The split follows two rules: things you cannot be without go in the cabin, and anything that is a liquid, gel, cream, spray or sharp has to meet the security rules for the bag it travels in.

Cabin bag. NaTHNaC advises keeping regularly used medicines in hand luggage, with an extra supply in the hold in case a bag is lost on the way. The UK government’s guidance says tablets and capsules, syringes and inhalers are allowed in both hand and hold luggage. The US Transportation Security Administration (TSA) says the same for pills: both carry-on and checked bags are allowed. The practical reading is simple: put a full set of your essential medicines, and a day or two of everything else, in the cabin.

Hold bag. Put the bulk of the kit here: larger bottles, spare plasters, the extra supply of regular medicines, and the sterile medical kit if you are taking one. NaTHNaC says a sterile kit with items such as needles, syringes and gloves, meant to be handed to a nurse or doctor in an emergency, should be packed in the hold rather than hand luggage. Scissors can go either way: TSA allows scissors in carry-on if the blade is less than 4 inches from the pivot point, and in checked bags if the blades are sheathed or securely wrapped. Packing small rounded-tip scissors avoids the question.

Sun cream and sprays in the hold. For the US, TSA says that checked bags may hold no more than 70 oz (2 kg) or 68 fl oz (2 litres) of medicinal and toiletry articles, including aerosols, per person, with no single container above 18 oz (0.5 kg) or 17 fl oz (500 ml). Aerosol nozzles must be protected with a cap or cover. Check your own airline’s page as well, as carriers can add limits.

Liquids, gels and creams: the 100ml rule and its medical exemptions

Most medicine kits include something that counts as a liquid: sun cream, antiseptic cream, hand sanitiser, eye drops, rehydration solution, insect repellent. In hand luggage the general limits are:

Country Limit in hand luggage Medical exemption
United States (TSA) Containers of 3.4 oz (100 ml) or less, carried in a quart-sized bag Medications are exempt. TSA allows larger amounts of medically necessary liquids, gels and aerosols in reasonable quantities for your trip, which you must declare to the officer at the checkpoint
United Kingdom At most airports, containers of no more than 100ml; some airports may allow up to 2 litres; where a bag is required it must hold no more than a litre and measure about 20cm x 20cm Containers up to 2 litres are allowed through security at all UK airports if for essential medical purposes. Proof (a doctor’s letter or prescription copy) is needed only for liquid medicine in containers over 100ml

Three practical points follow. First, the UK page lists creams, gels, pastes, sprays and contact lens solution as liquids, so treat sun cream, antiseptic cream and hand sanitiser the same way and keep them at 100ml in the cabin. Second, a medical exemption covers medicine, not toiletries: your 250ml bottle of sun cream does not qualify and belongs in the hold. Third, the UK government notes that the rules depend on the airport and that you should check the airport you fly from and any you pass through, including on the return. Staff may open containers to check them. Our airport liquids guide has more on carry-on rules, and a clear quart-size toiletry bag keeps the screening step quick.

Ice packs are a separate case. The UK government says ice packs in hand luggage are a matter for the airport, so contact them first; they are allowed in hold luggage.

Prescription medicines: packaging, paperwork and quantities

This is where the real trouble sits. Official sources agree on the basics:

  • Original packaging. The CDC recommends carrying prescriptions preferably in their original bottle, and NaTHNaC advises keeping medicines and medical equipment in the original labelled packaging. Do not decant pills into unlabelled containers to save space.
  • Copies and a letter. The CDC recommends bringing copies of prescriptions. NaTHNaC suggests a copy of the prescription plus a letter from the prescriber that gives the actual drug names rather than brand names, which can help at border checks or if you need a replacement abroad.
  • Enough, in two places. Take enough for the trip plus a margin for delays, split between cabin and hold bags, as above.
  • Check the destination. The CDC notes that some countries restrict the medicines you can bring and advises checking with the embassy of your destination. NaTHNaC adds that some medicines sold over the counter in the UK, such as nasal decongestants containing pseudoephedrine, are illegal in some countries.

Controlled medicines

If any of your medicines contains a controlled drug (Japan’s list, for instance, names codeine, morphine, oxycodone and methylphenidate), the paperwork matters more. The UK government’s guidance on travelling with controlled medicines says:

  • Keep the medicine in your hand luggage when entering or leaving the UK, as officials may take it if you cannot prove it was prescribed for you.
  • Ask your doctor or pharmacist whether your medicine contains a controlled drug. If it does, check the destination’s rules with its embassy, and carry a prescription or a letter from your doctor.
  • Anyone bringing medicine into the UK may bring only up to 3 months’ supply. Non-residents need a letter showing their name, travel dates, the medicine with quantity, dose and strength, and the prescriber’s signature.
  • Schedule 1 drugs need a licence, which is limited to research and other special purposes.

If you are a US traveller, the CDC points you to the embassy of your destination and to the State Department’s customs and import restrictions pages. We could not open the State Department’s own medication page for this guide (it blocked automated access), so check it directly.

Japan as an example of how strict it can be

Japan is a useful example because it is a popular destination where several everyday medicines are restricted. The Embassy of Japan in the UK says there are two kinds of approval that might be needed:

  1. Narcotics. You must obtain permission from the relevant Narcotics Control Department irrespective of the amount, for narcotics such as codeine and morphine.
  2. Yunyu Kakunin-sho (import certificate). For medicines that are not narcotics, you need this certificate if you intend to bring more than 1 month’s supply.

The embassy gives examples of non-narcotic medicines that can be taken in up to a one-month supply without permission, including paracetamol, ibuprofen, aspirin, inhalers containing salbutamol, statins, blood pressure tablets, antibiotics, vitamins and eye drops.

The Narcotics Control Department of Japan’s Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare lists codeine as a narcotic and pseudoephedrine as a “stimulants’ raw material”, both needing permission before you enter. It also says some medicines cannot be imported even if they are prescription medicines in your own country. Applications need a medical certificate from the prescriber (with your name, address, the specific reason you take the medicine, a list with doses and strengths, signature, and an issue date within 3 months), and must be made at least 14 days before travel. The Embassy of Japan in the US says the Yunyu Kakunin-sho must be applied for and received before leaving and declared to the customs officer on arrival.

In practice that means a cold remedy with pseudoephedrine or a prescription painkiller with codeine, ordinary items at home, can create a problem at the Japanese border. This is an example, not a rule for every country: other destinations restrict different medicines, so look up your own country pair and ask the embassy if the answer is unclear. What to buy at your destination instead

You do not have to carry everything. Items that are bulky, heavy, restricted or easy to replace are usually better bought on arrival:

  • Sun cream, insect repellent and hand sanitiser in large bottles. These are heavy, and anything over 100ml has to go in the hold. Buying a bottle at the destination avoids the liquids rule altogether.
  • Plasters, bandages, tape and blister pads. Easy to find in most pharmacies and convenience stores.
  • Rehydration sachets and basic stomach remedies in places with well-supplied pharmacies. Check the ingredient list or ask the pharmacist, since brand names and formulations vary.
  • A replacement for something that ran out or was lost. A local doctor or pharmacist can advise, and your copy of the prescription and the generic drug names will help.

Two cautions. NaTHNaC says medicines widely available over the counter at home may be difficult to find or unavailable in other countries, and that counterfeit drugs are an issue in some regions; it discourages buying medication overseas where quality cannot be assured, in part for that reason. So buy basic first aid items freely, but take your regular medicines and anything where a substitute could be unsuitable with you. And remember that a medicine prescribed for one person may be unsuitable for another, so never swap with a travelling companion.

Your kit and your insurance

A kit treats minor problems; insurance is for the big ones. Both the CDC and NaTHNaC list insurance documents among the paperwork to carry, so put your policy number and the insurer’s emergency line on your paperwork page. Before you go

  • Check the carry-on liquids rules for your departure airport and any connection.
  • Ask a pharmacist or doctor about your regular medicines, and whether any contain a controlled drug.
  • Look up your destination’s medicine rules with the embassy or official import authority, and allow at least two weeks if a permit is required (Japan’s Narcotics Control Department asks for 14 days).
  • Pack a day or two of essentials in the cabin, and a spare supply in the hold. If a bag goes missing, our lost luggage recovery guide covers the first 48 hours.
  • Sort your phone connection before landing; a travel eSIM lets you reach a pharmacy, clinic or your insurer from the airport. Our packing-cube system guide helps keep the pouch easy to reach.

Frequently asked questions

What should be in a travel medical kit?

Core items are pain and fever relief such as paracetamol or ibuprofen, oral rehydration salts and a diarrhoea remedy, an antihistamine, plasters and blister pads, antiseptic wipes, tweezers and a thermometer, plus your own regular medicines and a copy of your prescriptions. UK travel health service NaTHNaC says the kit should be tailored to you, your destination and your plans, and prescription-only items such as antibiotics belong in it only if a professional has prescribed them.

Can I take medicines over 100ml in hand luggage?

Yes, in both the US and UK there are medical exemptions. TSA allows larger amounts of medically necessary liquids in reasonable quantities if you declare them at the checkpoint. The UK government allows containers up to 2 litres for essential medical purposes and says proof, such as a doctor's letter or prescription copy, is needed for liquid medicine over 100ml. Sun cream and other toiletries are not covered by the medical exemption, so keep them at 100ml in the cabin or pack them in the hold.

Should medicines go in carry-on or checked luggage?

Keep what you cannot do without in your cabin bag, in original labelled packaging, and put an extra supply in the hold in case a bag is lost or delayed, as NaTHNaC advises. Pills are allowed in both cabin and hold bags in the US and UK. A sterile medical kit with needles and syringes should go in the hold. Bulky liquids and larger bottles also belong in the hold, subject to airline and TSA limits for checked toiletries.

Do I need a doctor's letter for prescription medicines abroad?

It is strongly advised. The CDC recommends copies of prescriptions, and NaTHNaC suggests a letter from the prescriber giving the actual drug names. For controlled medicines the UK government says to carry a prescription or doctor's letter and to check the destination's rules with its embassy. Some countries require formal permission as well, so check the official source for your destination.

Which common medicines are restricted in Japan?

Japan requires permission in advance for narcotics such as codeine and morphine irrespective of the amount, and its Narcotics Control Department lists pseudoephedrine as a stimulants' raw material that also needs permission. For other medicines, more than one month's supply needs a Yunyu Kakunin-sho import certificate. Applications should be made at least 14 days before travel. Other countries restrict different medicines, so check your own destination.

What can I buy at my destination instead of packing it?

Bulky or easily replaced items such as large bottles of sun cream, insect repellent, plasters and basic first aid supplies are usually easy to buy on arrival. NaTHNaC warns that familiar medicines may be hard to find or unavailable abroad and that counterfeit drugs are a problem in some regions, so pack your regular and prescribed medicines and ask a local pharmacist about anything else. This is general guidance, not medical advice.
