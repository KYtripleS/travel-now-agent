The platform matters less than you’d think. Several of Tokyo’s best-reviewed food tours are sold on more than one site at once, so the question is rarely “which site has the good tour?” It’s “where is the same tour cheapest today, and what exactly is included?” On 24 September 2026, a tour sold as “Shinjuku: Food Tour – 15 Dishes at 4 Eateries” was US$63.95 on Klook in an autumn sale and from ¥12,000 on Viator. Same title, same format, different price.

What really decides your night is three other things: the neighbourhood (neon alleys in Shinjuku, old-town snacks in Asakusa, a morning at Tsukiji), the time of day, and what the price covers, from two drinks to free-flow, or nothing but the guide. This guide compares the platforms, then the tours, so you can choose both.

Viator is where the volume is. More than 300 Tokyo food tours, and the review counts to match: the top Shinjuku food tour (13 dishes at four eateries with two drinks, from ¥13,000) had 9,843 reviews when we checked. If you want to read a hundred honest accounts before you commit an evening, this is the place.

Klook is where to check the price of a tour you have already chosen. It sells many of the same headline tours, and during its sales the difference is real: the 15-dish, four-eatery Shinjuku tour was US$63.95 with 30% off, against ¥12,000 on Viator. Klook also has the cheapest well-reviewed format we found, a Shinjuku local bar and izakaya crawl at US$37.79 (814 reviews, 4.9 out of 5). At that price, read the package details to see what food and drink are included before comparing it with the all-in tours. One quirk: Klook’s “food tours” page also lists the Pokémon Café and a muscle-bar show, so use the street-food filter or search by neighbourhood. Check Tokyo food tours on Klook.

KKday, excellent elsewhere in Asia (see our Klook vs KKday comparison), is thin for Tokyo food: 14 listings in its food-tour category, several of them day trips rather than food tours, and the food ones mostly without reviews, at US$89 to US$170. The exception worth a look is its bar-hopping evening in Kichijoji’s Harmonica Yokocho, a warren of tiny bars you won’t find on the bigger lists. See it on KKday.

Shinjuku after dark: the classic

This is the tour most first-timers mean. Three or four small places around Omoide Yokocho, Golden Gai and Kabukicho, yakitori and sake, and a guide who knows which six-seat counter has room at 7pm. The well-reviewed versions run about three hours for ¥12,000 to ¥16,880 and include the food and at least two drinks; one popular bar-hopping format includes free-flow drinks and a full dinner. If you stay in Shinjuku, you can walk back to your hotel afterwards (our Tokyo stay guide has options on both sides of the station).

Tsukiji in the morning: go early, come hungry

The wholesale market moved to Toyosu in 2018, but Tsukiji’s outer market, the streets of stalls around it, is still the best food walk in the city. Tours range from a 90-minute guided walk from about ¥2,000 to three hours with five tastings included for ¥15,000. At the cheap end, check what food the price includes; the short walks are good value if you mostly want a guide to explain what you’re looking at, and you can buy the rest yourself. The tuna auction itself is at Toyosu, before dawn, and the specialist tours that get you in cost ¥19,000 to ¥23,400.

Asakusa: old-town snacks

Asakusa’s food tours are shorter and cheaper, around two to two and a half hours for about ¥11,250 to ¥11,500, and lean towards the street snacks and small izakaya of old Tokyo: monjayaki, yakisoba, sake, and a fish-shaped taiyaki to finish. One listing says clearly that extras are paid in cash, which is common, so bring some. It pairs well with an early Senso-ji visit and, if you like old coffee houses, the pancakes at Coffee Tengoku in our kissaten guide.

What the price really covers

Two tours at the same price can be very different evenings. Before you compare, check four things in the listing:

  1. Dishes and stops. “13 dishes at 3 eateries” and “15 dishes at 4 eateries” are both common. Count stops as well as dishes; one more stop means one more room and one more chef.
  2. Drinks. One or two included drinks is typical. Free-flow is rarer and usually costs more. Some crawls include very little, and you pay at each bar.
  3. Extras in cash. Anything beyond the set menu is usually paid on the spot, and small places often take cash only.
  4. Dietary needs. Vegetarian and no-pork requests can usually be met with notice, but the kitchens are other people’s. Arigato Travel, for example, adapts its Shibuya tour for vegetarian, gluten-free, no-seafood, no-pork and pescatarian diets with advance notice, and says plainly that it cannot guarantee allergy-free meals, because the food is prepared in third-party kitchens. If you have a serious allergy, message the operator before booking, whichever site you use.

Why the same tour costs different amounts

Operators set their own prices on each platform, and the platforms run their own sales and coupons on top. Viator shows prices in yen for many Tokyo tours, Klook in your chosen currency, so compare after converting and on the same date. The practical routine:

  1. Find the tour you want on Viator, where the reviews are.
  2. Search the same title on Klook and GetYourGuide.
  3. Book whichever is cheapest with free cancellation up to 24 hours. Most of the top listings we checked offered it, and it lets you move the evening if the weather or your jet lag decides otherwise.

Or do it yourself?

You can. Omoide Yokocho is open to anyone, and so is Tsukiji. What a guide buys you is the ordering, some context, and a seat in the small places that don’t have English menus or room for four strangers at once. For a first night in Tokyo it is worth it; by your fourth night you may enjoy finding your own counter. A good middle path is a cheap guided morning at Tsukiji, then evenings on your own. Keep your maps and translation working with a travel eSIM (Airalo is our default for Japan; our Japan eSIM guide compares it with Saily and the rest).

Before you book: five checks

  1. Free cancellation up to 24 hours. Standard on the top listings; don’t book one without it.
  2. The meeting point. Shinjuku Station has more than 200 exits, so screenshot the exact meeting spot.
  3. What’s included. Dishes, stops and drinks, as above.
  4. Group size. “Small group” can mean six people or fifteen; one reviewer counted fifteen on a popular Shinjuku crawl. Check the maximum in the listing if it matters to you.
  5. Your first night. Jet lag and a three-hour evening of food and drink don’t mix well. Book the tour for your second or third night.

Frequently asked questions

Is Klook or Viator better for Tokyo food tours?

Viator has more choice, with over 300 Tokyo food tours and review counts in the thousands. Klook sells many of the same tours and is often cheaper during its sales: on 24 September 2026 a Shinjuku tour sold as 15 dishes at 4 eateries was US$63.95 on Klook and from ¥12,000 on Viator. Choose the tour on Viator, then check the same title on Klook.

How much does a Tokyo food tour cost?

Shared evening food tours in Shinjuku typically cost ¥12,000 to ¥16,880 for about three hours, with 13 to 15 dishes and drinks included. Asakusa food tours are around ¥11,250 to ¥11,500 for two to two and a half hours. Tsukiji market walks range from about ¥2,000 to ¥15,000, and premium sushi and wagyu tours start around ¥28,000.

Does Klook or Viator offer more food tours in Tokyo?

Viator, by a wide margin: more than 300 Tokyo food tours when we checked in September 2026. Klook lists dozens, and its food-tour category also includes character cafés and shows, so filter by street food or search by neighbourhood.

Are Tokyo food tours worth it?

For a first night, usually yes. A guide handles the ordering, explains what you are eating and gets you into small izakaya with a handful of seats and no English menu. Once you are settled, Omoide Yokocho and Tsukiji are easy to explore on your own.

Can Tokyo food tours accommodate vegetarians or allergies?

Vegetarian, no-pork and no-seafood requests can often be met with advance notice, but food is cooked in the restaurants' own kitchens. Arigato Travel, for example, adapts its Shibuya evening tour for several diets but says it cannot guarantee allergy-free meals. With a serious allergy, message the operator before booking.

Can you still see the tuna auction in Tokyo?

Yes, at Toyosu Market, where the wholesale market moved from Tsukiji in 2018. The auction is before dawn; specialist tours that arrange access cost roughly ¥19,000 to ¥23,400, and breakfast is sometimes extra. Tsukiji's outer market remains the easier food walk.
