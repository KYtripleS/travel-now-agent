A top-end night in Tokyo is a choice between four things that room photos blur together: the view, the bath, the area and the tax. A room facing Mount Fuji and a room facing the next office tower can sit on the same corridor and carry very different prices. A hot-spring bath can mean a shared bath on the top floor, a spa you book by the hour, or a tub in the room. And Tokyo adds a per-person, per-night accommodation tax that is small today and changes on 1 April 2027, so it matters if your dates straddle the switch.

This guide covers four areas and ten properties at the top of the market, from a hot-spring ryokan tower in Otemachi to a hotel at the foot of Tokyo Tower. It is for one or two special nights, such as an anniversary, a honeymoon or a first trip to Japan done properly, and it answers “which hotel, and which kind of room, for whom”. The hotels are researched picks, chosen for location, what each property publishes and consistent guest feedback, and each was checked as operating in November 2026. No hotel pays to appear here. What makes a luxury bill in Tokyo different

Three lines turn up on a Tokyo luxury invoice that do not show on a headline rate.

The accommodation tax. Tokyo charges it per person per night. Until 31 March 2027 it is a flat ¥100 or ¥200 depending on the rate; from 1 April 2027 it is a percentage. Details and a worked example are in the next section.

Service charge and consumption tax. Many hotels quote rates with these added; the booking page says whether they are included. Count them before comparing two hotels.

Meals. City hotels sell rooms with or without breakfast. A ryokan sells a night with dinner and breakfast, usually fixed. HOSHINOYA Tokyo’s official pages list three restaurants but we could not confirm from them whether dinner and breakfast are part of the room price, so check the plan name before you pay.

Three kinds of bath, and why the word “private” needs care

  • A bath in your room. It is yours for the whole stay. Some hotels fit a deep stone or cypress tub (Aman Tokyo says its suites have stone furo baths; Hotel Chinzanso lists a room type with a cypress bath), a few have an open-air bath on a balcony, and none of the ten here publishes an in-room hot-spring bath that we could confirm.
  • A reservable private bath (kashikiri). A small bath you book for a set time with your own party, common in hot-spring towns, uncommon in city hotels. None of the ten lists one on the pages we read.
  • A shared bath (daiyokujo). One bath per gender, used by whoever is in at the time, with the usual rules: wash first, no swimsuits, and often a ban or restriction on visible tattoos. HOSHINOYA Tokyo and The Prince Park Tower Tokyo have this kind.

The Tokyo accommodation tax

Tokyo’s tax is set by the Tokyo Metropolitan Government and, per its Bureau of Taxation page, is payable by the guest. At a hotel or ryokan front desk it may be collected separately from the room price.

Stay date Rate, per person per night
Until 31 March 2027, room charge under ¥10,000 No tax
Until 31 March 2027, ¥10,000 to under ¥15,000 ¥100
Until 31 March 2027, ¥15,000 or more ¥200
From 1 April 2027, under ¥13,000 per person per night No tax
From 1 April 2027, ¥13,000 or more 3% of the accommodation charge per person per night

We looked for a change in 2026 and found none to the rates: the metropolitan page shows the ordinance was amended, and the new 3% scheme begins on 1 April 2027. From that date the page also extends the tax to simple lodging and private rental homes.

Worked example (our arithmetic, not an official figure). Take a luxury room that costs ¥80,000 a night for two people, so ¥40,000 per person, for three nights. Today that is ¥200 per person per night: ¥400 a night, ¥1,200 for the stay. The same room on or after 1 April 2027 is 3% of ¥40,000, which is ¥1,200 per person per night: ¥2,400 a night, ¥7,200 for three nights. On the old schedule the tax stays at ¥200 however high the rate goes; on the new one it scales with the rate. The ¥80,000 is an illustration, not a quote. Whether service charge or meals count in the base is set by the ordinance rather than the page we read, so ask the hotel what its bill includes.

Getting in from the airports

These are the operators’ published fares.

  • From Narita: the Narita Express to Tokyo Station is ¥3,140 in an ordinary car, with the fastest train taking 53 minutes. Shinagawa, Shibuya and Shinjuku are ¥3,330. The Keisei Skyliner to Keisei-Ueno is ¥1,280 base fare plus a ¥1,300 Skyliner fee, about 40 minutes.
  • From Haneda: the Tokyo Monorail from Terminal 3 to Hamamatsucho is ¥520, with trains on average every five minutes.

The Narita Express stops at Tokyo Station, which suits Shangri-La, Bvlgari and the Marunouchi hotels. Our airport guide compares every train and bus, and our first-day plan covers what to do between a morning landing and a mid-afternoon check-in.

    Marunouchi, Otemachi and Tokyo Station: easy to reach, calm at night

    This is the business district beside Tokyo Station, and for a short luxury stay it is the least tiring base: the Narita Express and the Shinkansen are close, the Imperial Palace gardens are a short walk, and nights are quiet. The trade-off is that it empties out in the evening; you go to Ginza or Nihonbashi for dinner.

    Nihonbashi and Hibiya: old merchants’ Tokyo and the palace moat

    Nihonbashi is the old commercial heart, with department stores and long-established shops, and it sits a short ride or walk from both Tokyo Station and Ginza. Hibiya, just south of the palace, is the formal hotel district. Both suit a first trip that wants polish without Shinjuku’s volume.

    Where to stay in Nihonbashi and Hibiya

    • Mandarin Oriental, Tokyo, splurge. 157 rooms and 22 suites in Nihonbashi, with a spa that has an indoor vitality pool and water lounges, and a page for connecting and family rooms. Choose it for a spa day and rooms that connect. Watch out: the site speaks of panoramic views but names none; ask for a higher floor if the skyline matters. Check rates on Booking.com →
    • Imperial Hotel Tokyo, splurge. The grand old name beside Hibiya Park and the Imperial Palace. Its Kids Friendly page lists free children’s amenities (pyjamas, slippers, a baby bath for the youngest), rentals of cots and bed rails, and a paid Baby Room for ages 1 month to 8 years. Choose it for a family stay at the top of the market. Watch out: the Baby Room is hourly and charged, and the page names no family room categories, so ask which rooms take an extra bed.     Toranomon, Akasaka and Shiba: views of Fuji and Tokyo Tower

    If the room is the point, this south-western side is where the views are: Tokyo Tower close up, a long view toward Fuji on a clear day, and tall towers set around parks. Fuji is a winter and early-morning view, and it is never guaranteed, so treat a Fuji room as a chance rather than a promise.

    Where to stay in Toranomon, Akasaka and Shiba

    • The Ritz-Carlton, Tokyo, splurge. 245 rooms in the top nine floors of the 53-storey Midtown Tower, with arrival on the 45th floor and, per its site, views of the Imperial Palace Garden, Tokyo Bay and Mount Fuji. It has an indoor pool, a hot tub and a spa. Choose it for the best-advertised Fuji outlook on this list. Watch out: guests must be at least 18 to check in, and a room is not guaranteed a Fuji view, so ask for a high floor facing west. Check rates on Booking.com →

Choose it for a calmer, more Japanese-feeling base in a central position. Watch out: its site does not state a room count or a Fuji view, so ask for the room type before you book. Check rates on Booking.com →

At the foot of Tokyo Tower in Shiba Park, with 263 Tower View Rooms (some looking up at the tower, some level with it, and premium rooms above its illuminated crown), and a natural hot spring in its spa. Choose it for Tokyo Tower from the bed and an onsen in the building. Watch out: the spa is for guests aged 20 or over, and guests with tattoos may not be allowed in certain facilities.     A garden away from the centre

    Bunkyo is a quiet residential ward north of the centre. A stay here trades the station for green space: roughly 25 minutes by taxi from Tokyo Station or Ginza, and about ten minutes on foot from Edogawabashi Station, according to the hotel.

    • Hotel Chinzanso Tokyo, comfortable. A hotel set amid gardens, with a room list that includes a Prime Superior Room with a cypress bath, a Japanese Suite and a Prime Deluxe Garden Suite with bunk beds for four. Choose it for a family stay with space and a garden, or a tatami-style room at a hotel that is not a ryokan. Watch out: it is not central, and a taxi or train is part of every outing.     How to book a luxury stay in Tokyo

    1. Count the tax and the extras. Add the accommodation tax from the table above, and check whether the price shown includes service charge and consumption tax.
    2. Pick the room, not just the hotel. The view and the bath are properties of the room type. Ask for the floor and direction in the booking notes, and treat a Fuji or Tokyo Tower view as a request rather than a guarantee.
    3. Check the season. Cherry blossom weeks, Golden Week, late autumn and the year-end holiday are the tightest. The same room can cost far more than on a quiet weeknight.
    4. Check size and beds. Compare square metres, not photos, and confirm bed types and the child policy for your party.
    5. Read the cancellation line. A flexible rate lets you rebook if plans move; our comparison of hotel booking sites shows how the main sites handle it.
    6. Plan the arrival. If you land before check-in, our luggage guide lists where to leave bags.

    Frequently asked questions

    Which luxury hotel in Tokyo has a hot spring?

    HOSHINOYA Tokyo in Otemachi lists a natural hot spring from 1,500 metres underground (one onsen and one public bath), and The Prince Park Tower Tokyo in Shiba lists a natural hot spring in its spa, open to guests aged 20 or over. Both baths are shared, not in the room, and tattoo rules apply at the Prince.

    Which Tokyo luxury hotels have Tokyo Tower or Mount Fuji views?

    The Prince Park Tower Tokyo advertises 263 Tower View Rooms at the foot of Tokyo Tower. The Ritz-Carlton, Tokyo lists Mount Fuji among the views from its top nine floors of Midtown Tower. Views depend on the room and the weather, so ask for a floor and direction; neither is guaranteed.

    Which luxury hotels in Tokyo suit children?

    The Imperial Hotel Tokyo lists free children's amenities, cot and bed-rail rentals and a paid Baby Room for ages 1 month to 8 years. Mandarin Oriental, Tokyo has a connecting and family rooms page, and Hotel Chinzanso Tokyo lists a garden suite with bunk beds for four. The Ritz-Carlton requires guests to be at least 18 to check in.

    How much is the accommodation tax in a Tokyo luxury hotel?

    Until 31 March 2027 it is ¥200 per person per night for rooms of ¥15,000 or more per person (¥100 from ¥10,000 to under ¥15,000). From 1 April 2027 it is 3% of the accommodation charge per person per night, with stays under ¥13,000 exempt. On a ¥40,000 per person night that is ¥200 today and ¥1,200 from April 2027 (our arithmetic).

    Can I stay in a ryokan in central Tokyo?

    HOSHINOYA Tokyo in Otemachi is a ryokan tower with 84 rooms, tatami lounges, check-in at 3:00 pm and check-out at 12:00 pm. Its bath is shared rather than in the room. We could not confirm its tattoo policy, minimum age or meal plan from its pages, so ask before booking.

    Which luxury hotel is closest to the Shinkansen?

    Shangri-La Tokyo says it is about a one-minute walk from Tokyo Station, where the Tokaido Shinkansen and the Narita Express stop. Bvlgari Hotel Tokyo sits on the 40th to 45th floors of Tokyo Midtown Yaesu on the station's Yaesu side.
