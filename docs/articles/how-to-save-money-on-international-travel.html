How to Save Money on International Travel: 7 Levers That Actually Move the Number

By Casey, Gently Yonder editor

The handful of decisions that genuinely cut an international trip's cost: how you pay abroad (and why to always choose the local currency), eSIM vs roaming, flexible flight dates, pass arithmetic, luggage and transfers, insurance you already own, and referral credits.

Updated 2026-09-13 · 9 min read

Build My Trip Checklist All guides

Answer first: most travel money-saving advice is noise. Only a handful of decisions change your total by a meaningful amount, and they are usually not the ones people obsess over. Ranked by how much they actually save on a typical two-week international trip: flights and dates (the largest single line), how you pay abroad (quiet, but it taxes every purchase you make), mobile data (the easiest win), pre-booked activities and passes, transfers and luggage, not double-paying for insurance, and finally referral credits (small, but genuinely free money). Below is each lever, honestly weighted — including the ones I think deserve none of your attention.

1. How you pay abroad — the leak almost nobody sees

Start here, because unlike everything else on this list, this one taxes every single purchase of your trip. Three separate charges quietly stack up abroad:

  • A poor exchange rate. Airport exchange counters and many high-street bank rates are among the worst you will find anywhere.
  • A foreign transaction fee. Many cards add roughly 1.5–3% to every overseas purchase, invisibly, on top of the rate.
  • Dynamic currency conversion (DCC) — the most avoidable of the three. When a card terminal or foreign ATM asks whether you’d like to be charged in your home currency or the local one, choosing your home currency hands the rate-setting to the merchant’s processor, typically at a noticeably worse rate. Always choose the local currency. This habit costs nothing, takes one second, and applies to every transaction you make.

The structural fix is a multi-currency account or travel card that converts near the mid-market rate and doesn’t add a foreign-transaction fee — Wise and Revolut are the two most travellers use. We have no affiliate relationship with either; this is simply what the arithmetic favours. If you exchange your home currency repeatedly on every trip, this is usually the largest recurring saving on this entire list, and it compounds across every future trip you take.

While you’re planning the cash side, how much physical currency to actually carry varies enormously by country — how much cash to bring to Japan is a worked example of getting that balance right.

2. Mobile data: stop roaming, start using an eSIM

This is the easiest win on the list because it takes ten minutes and is a one-time decision.

Carrier roaming is typically billed per day, and a single day of it often costs more than an entire trip’s worth of eSIM data. An eSIM is just a profile you install on your phone before you fly — no shop to find on arrival, no deposit, no swapping out your home SIM. You can compare country and regional eSIM plans here, or check an alternative provider’s pricing before you commit — plans for most destinations cost a few dollars for a week.

Two honest cautions so you don’t overpay in the other direction:

  • “Unlimited” rarely means unlimited speed. Almost every unlimited plan throttles after a daily high-speed allowance. We broke the fine print down in do “unlimited” travel eSIMs really give you unlimited data? — for most travellers a cheaper fixed-data plan is the better buy.
  • Timing matters. Many plans start their clock on activation, not on arrival, so activating too early quietly wastes days you paid for. See when to activate a travel eSIM.

If you’re still deciding between formats, eSIM vs a physical SIM card covers when a local SIM genuinely still wins.

3. Flights: flexibility beats tricks

Flights are usually the single largest line on the budget, which is exactly why so much folklore has grown around them. The honest version: there is no reliable secret day or hour to book. What demonstrably works is duller:

  • Move your dates, not your search. Shifting departure by a day or two, or travelling outside the local peak season, moves the price far more than any booking trick.
  • Check nearby airports and consider one-way pairs on different carriers.
  • Set an alert early rather than refreshing daily. You want to be notified of a drop, not to spend three weeks watching one.

When you’re ready to compare, you can search flexible dates across airlines here and let the calendar view show you what a day’s flexibility is actually worth on your route.

4. Activities and attractions: book ahead, then check the pass arithmetic

Pre-booking major attractions usually beats the gate price, and for popular sites it also protects you from a sold-out day — which is a real cost, just not a financial one. You can compare activity and attraction prices here, cross-check a second platform (prices for the same tour genuinely differ between them), or book museum and attraction tickets directly.

One honest caution about city passes: they’re sold as obvious savings, but they only pay off above a certain number of paid attractions per day. Before buying, add up what you’d actually visit — not what the pass covers. If your trip is slow-paced, a pass often costs more than paying as you go, and it quietly pressures you into a rushed itinerary to “get your money’s worth.”

5. The small on-the-ground costs that add up

These are individually minor and collectively significant:

  • Luggage on awkward days. On an arrival or departure day, paying for an extra hotel night just to store bags is far more expensive than a locker. You can find luggage storage near stations and city centres here and reclaim a whole day you’d otherwise lose.
  • Airport transfers. Work out the train or bus option before you land, while you still have patience and signal. The taxi decision made while exhausted at 11pm is the expensive one.
  • Where you stay changes what you spend. A room near a station can cost slightly more and still save money overall through cheaper daily transport and fewer taxis. Hotel booking sites compared covers which platforms show the genuine all-in price, including the local lodging taxes that get added at check-out.

6. Don’t double-pay for insurance

Travel insurance is worth having — but a surprising number of people buy cover they already own. Many premium credit cards bundle trip-delay, rental-car, and sometimes emergency medical benefits. Read your card’s benefits guide first, then insure only the genuine gaps, which for most people are the large medical and evacuation numbers.

The reasoning behind that rule — insure what would hurt, self-insure what wouldn’t — is laid out in is travel insurance a rip-off?, and travel insurance compared lines up providers on what actually matters. When you want a figure for your own dates, you can get a quick quote and check it against what your card already covers before buying anything.

7. Referral credits: small, but genuinely free

Most travel services — money apps, booking platforms, activity sites, eSIM providers — run refer-a-friend programmes where both people get credit once the new user completes a first qualifying booking or transfer. Unlike most “hacks”, this one is legitimate by design: the companies actively want you to share it.

Two honest limits. First, the amounts change constantly and vary by country, so treat any figure you read online as approximate and check the current offer before counting on it. Second, this is not passive income — someone has to actually sign up and transact. Used sensibly across the services you were going to use anyway, though, it’s free money, and the credits usually land exactly where you’ll spend them.

One thing to avoid: using your own affiliate link on your own purchase. Almost every affiliate programme forbids this, and it risks your account and any earnings. Referral programmes are for referring other people; that’s the distinction.

What is not worth your attention

Balance matters, so here’s the counterweight:

  • Refreshing flight prices in private browsing. The belief that repeated searching inflates your fare is widespread; there’s no good evidence it reliably works that way. Set an alert instead.
  • Extreme frugality that costs you the trip. Saving a modest sum by staying an hour outside the city, then paying for transport and losing two hours a day, is usually a bad trade in both money and experience.
  • Chasing small cashback while losing several percent to DCC. Fix the structural leak in section 1 first; it dwarfs the rest.

The honest summary

If you only do three things: choose the local currency at every terminal, replace roaming with an eSIM, and be flexible on dates rather than clever about booking times. Those three cover most of the realistically achievable savings on an international trip. Everything else on this list is worth doing, but it’s refinement — and refinement is only worth your energy once the structural leaks are closed.

Frequently asked questions

What saves the most money on international travel? Fixing that compounds across every trip you take.

Should I pay in my home currency or the local currency abroad? Always choose the local currency. Choosing your home currency triggers dynamic currency conversion (DCC), which lets the merchant’s processor set the exchange rate, typically at a worse one than your card would give you.
