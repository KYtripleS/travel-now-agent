Plugs and voltage by country

By Casey, Gently Yonder editor

Plug types, voltage and frequency for 216 countries and territories, and a checker that tells you whether you need an adapter, a converter, or neither.

The quick answer

Most phone, laptop and camera chargers say INPUT 100–240 V. Those need only a plug adapter, anywhere in this table. A voltage converter matters only for single-voltage appliances, mostly hair dryers and straighteners, when you travel between a 100–127 V country and a 220–240 V one.

Do you need an adapter?

The checker counts a plug as fitting only where the socket is built for it: a Type A plug fits a Type B socket, and a two-pin Type C plug fits Type E, F, J, K, L and N sockets. Hotels vary, and some keep universal sockets or spare adapters at reception, but do not count on it.

Adapter or converter?

A plug adapter changes only the shape, so your plug fits the socket. A voltage converter changes the electricity. Look at the small print on each charger: 100–240 V means it works on any mains supply in this table and needs only an adapter. A single number, such as 120 V or 230 V, means it is built for one supply.

Plugging a 120 V-only appliance into a 230 V socket can destroy it within seconds. The other way round, a 230 V appliance on 100–127 V usually runs weakly or not at all. 220 V and 230 V, or 110 V and 120 V, count as the same supply for travel purposes.

Frequency (50 or 60 Hz) rarely matters for chargers. It can make motors run slightly faster or slower and older plug-in clocks gain or lose time.

Plug types A to O

  • Type A. Two flat parallel pins, no earth. North and Central America and Japan. Japanese plugs have two equal pins, so they fit US sockets; US plugs with one wider pin may not fit older Japanese sockets.
  • Type B. Type A plus a round earth pin. It fits Type B sockets only; a two-pin Type A plug fits a Type B socket.
  • Type C. The Europlug: two round pins, no earth. Thin enough to fit most round-pin sockets (Types E, F, J, K, L and N), which makes it the most widely usable plug in the world.
  • Type D. Three large round pins in a triangle. India, Nepal, Sri Lanka and parts of Africa.
  • Type E. Two round pins, with the earth pin sticking out of the socket. France, Belgium, Poland, Czechia, Slovakia. Most plugs sold there are E/F hybrids that fit both.
  • Type F. Schuko: two round pins with earth clips on the sides. Germany and most of continental Europe, also South Korea and Russia.
  • Type G. Three rectangular pins, and a fuse in the plug. The UK, Ireland, Malta, Cyprus, Singapore, Hong Kong, Malaysia and much of the Gulf.
  • Type H. Israel's plug. Older sockets take flat pins in a V; newer ones take round pins and also accept Type C.
  • Type I. Two flat pins set in a V, often with an earth pin. Australia, New Zealand, China, Argentina and several Pacific islands.
  • Type J. Switzerland and Liechtenstein: three round pins. Swiss sockets also take Type C.
  • Type K. Denmark and Greenland: two round pins and a half-round earth pin. The sockets also take Type C.
  • Type L. Italy: three round pins in a row, in a 10 A and a thicker 16 A size. The sockets also take Type C.
  • Type M. A larger version of Type D. South Africa, Lesotho, Eswatini, and some sockets in India and the Gulf.
  • Type N. Brazil's standard, adopted by South Africa for new sockets: three round pins. The sockets also take Type C.
  • Type O. Thailand's national plug (TIS 166-2549), three round pins. The IEC table does not list it; Thai sockets are usually built to take Type A and C as well.

Where this data comes from

Plug types, voltage and frequency come from the World Plugs listing of the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC), the body that publishes the international plug and socket standards. The IEC retired that guide in 2026 and now points travellers to adapter makers and travel websites. We read the version saved by the Internet Archive on 19 January 2026, which matches the November 2025 copy country for country.

We checked every row against Wikipedia’s Mains electricity by country. In 33 rows it lists plug types that the IEC does not; the Note column says which. Where the two disagree, expect the IEC types in hotels and carry a universal adapter. The IEC lists some voltages at 220 V that are now nominally 230 V, and the reverse; for travel the difference does not matter.

Country names follow current usage (Eswatini, North Macedonia, Türkiye). If you spot an error, tell us and we will log the fix on our corrections page. Read how we verify.
