Kissaten in Tokyo: Nine Old Coffee Houses Worth the Detour (2026)

By Casey, Gently Yonder editor

What a kissaten is, how to behave in one, and nine old Tokyo coffee houses still going in 2026, from Café Paulista (1911) and Café de l'Ambre's aged coffee to Ladrio's Vienna coffee and Coffee Tengoku's pancakes.

Last updated 25 Sep 2026 · 10 min read

Build My Trip Checklist All guides

There is a particular quiet inside an old Tokyo kissaten. It isn’t silence. It’s the tick of a wall clock, a siphon bubbling behind the counter, a newspaper page turning two tables away, and nobody in any hurry at all. In a city that runs to the minute, these rooms keep their own time, and an hour in one can reset a whole travel day.

A kissaten (喫茶店, literally a “tea-drinking shop”) is Japan’s old-style coffee house: table service, coffee brewed cup by cup, a short menu of toast, spaghetti and pudding that has barely changed in fifty years, and a room that looks the way it did when it opened. They are not chain cafés and not the new specialty-coffee bars, though they taught both a great deal. This guide covers what to expect, how to behave, and nine places in Tokyo where the old way is still very much alive, each checked as open in September 2026.

What makes a kissaten a kissaten

Japan’s first Western-style coffee house, Kahiichakan, opened in Ueno in 1888. Its founder, Tei Eikei, had fallen for the coffee houses of London and built a salon with books, writing paper and billiards. It was far ahead of its time and closed within a few years, but the idea stayed.

By the 1920s, “café” in Tokyo had come to mean something else: places with hostesses and alcohol. Coffee houses that served only coffee and light food began calling themselves jun-kissa, “pure” kissaten, to make the difference clear. The word survives, and today it signals an old-school, coffee-first room that has kept its character.

What you’ll usually find inside:

  • Coffee brewed to order, often by siphon or by hand drip, with a house blend at the top of the menu and a list of single origins below it.
  • The Showa-era menu: thick toast, Napolitan spaghetti in ketchup, a firm custard pudding, and cream soda, a violently green melon soda topped with ice cream.
  • Morning sets (モーニング): toast, egg and coffee for little more than the coffee alone, served until late morning in many places.
  • A room that time forgot, on purpose: dark wood, velvet chairs, stained glass, a clock that chimes.

How to behave in a kissaten

None of this is complicated, but a few quiet customs make the visit easier for everyone, you included.

  • One order per person. Most kissaten expect everyone at the table to order at least a drink. They are small rooms, and seats are the business.
  • Bring cash. Many older places take cash only. Keep a few ¥1,000 notes handy; our guide to cash in Japan explains how much to carry.
  • Keep your voice low. People come here to read, think and do very little. Match the room.
  • Ask before photographing the interior or the staff. Many don’t mind; some do, and a sign at the counter will often tell you.
  • Don’t plan to work. Expect no Wi-Fi and few sockets, and don’t expect to stay three hours with a laptop over one coffee.
  • Check the smoking sign. Tokyo’s 2020 ordinance bans smoking in cafés with staff, but a few kissaten still allow it, usually because they sell tobacco at the counter and registered as a “smoking purpose” shop, or because they have no employees. The law requires a sign at the entrance, so read it before you sit if smoke bothers you.
  • Check the day. Old places keep their own hours and many close one day a week. Look up the current hours before you cross the city for one.

Ginza: three kinds of old

Ginza has the deepest bench of historic coffee houses in the city, all within a fifteen-minute walk of each other, and on weekend afternoons Chuo-dori closes to cars, which makes walking between them a pleasure.

Café Paulista opened in Ginza in 1911, making it Japan’s oldest surviving kissaten, and has been at its current address on Chuo-dori in Ginza 8-chome since 1970. It was founded by Ryo Mizuno, a leading organiser of early Japanese emigration to Brazil; “Paulista” means someone from São Paulo. Taisho-era writers such as Ryunosuke Akutagawa were regulars, and John Lennon and Yoko Ono came in the 1970s; a photograph of a cup they signed is on display. Order the house blend and sit with the history.

Café de l’Ambre, a few minutes away at Ginza 8-10-15, has served coffee since 1948 and very little else: one blend, around thirty single origins, many of them aged for ten to twenty years, and a handful of coffee sweets. Its founder, Ichiro Sekiguchi, kept working into his hundreds and died in 2018; the coffee is still made the way he set it down, by hand, cup by cup. If you have ever wondered what an aged coffee tastes like, this is where to find out. The nearest exit is A3 of Ginza Station, about six minutes away.

Tsubakiya Coffee takes a different kind of old. Since the late 1970s it has recreated the “Taisho romance” style of the 1910s and 1920s: dark wood, red velvet, staff in classic uniforms, coffee made in siphons and served in Royal Copenhagen cups. It is a recreation rather than an original, and it knows it; the room is a pleasure anyway. There are several branches in Ginza, Yurakucho and further afield.

Jimbocho: coffee among the books

Jimbocho is Tokyo’s secondhand-book district, and generations of students and writers have carried their finds into its coffee houses. Two of the best sit a minute apart in the lanes behind the main street.

Ladrio, open since 1949, takes its name from ladrillo, Spanish for brick, and the brick-and-timber room lives up to it. It is widely credited as the first café in Japan to serve Vienna coffee: hot coffee under a thick cap of whipped cream. Order that, obviously.

Sabouru, since 1955, is the opposite of hushed: a dim, cave-like room crowded with carved wood, masks, clocks and decades of graffiti, loved by students and anyone who likes a room with a story. The fresh strawberry juice is the famous order, and the cream sodas come in several colours. For Napolitan spaghetti, go next door to Sabouru 2.

Shinjuku: French toast and a station classic

Café Aaliya sits diagonally across from the Isetan department store at the Shinjuku 3-chome crossing, and it is known above all for one thing: thick French toast, cooked on a large copper plate so it is crisp outside and soft right through. It is a small room and it fills, so come outside lunch hours.

Berg, in the basement of Lumine Est at Shinjuku Station, is not a kissaten in the strict sense. It is a stand-up-and-sit-down café-bar serving coffee, beer, sausages and bread from 7am, and has done since 1970. But it has the same spirit: cheap, honest, and the same for decades. It is a good ten minutes between trains.

Asakusa and the north-east: pancakes and a pioneer

Coffee Tengoku, about five minutes’ walk from Senso-ji, is a tiny kissaten whose pancakes are branded with its name, 天国 (“heaven”), and made from batter mixed to order. It is on Tabelog’s 2026 list of the best kissaten, and it is small, so expect to queue. It pairs well with an early Senso-ji visit; our Asakusa guide covers the rest of the morning.

Café Bach, in the Nihonzutsumi neighbourhood near Minami-Senju, has roasted and brewed coffee since 1968. It is run by Mamoru and Fumiko Taguchi. He is known in the trade as a legendary roaster, and the business now runs a training centre to pass its methods on to the next generation. Jars of beans line the shelves behind the bar, Bach plays, and each cup is made with the care of a small ceremony. It is a short ride from Asakusa and worth planning a morning around.

A slow half-day, if you’d like one

  • Morning, Jimbocho: browse the book racks, then a Vienna coffee at Ladrio.
  • Late morning, Ginza: two stops on the Mita Line to Hibiya, beside Ginza. Look in at Café Paulista, then take an aged coffee at Café de l’Ambre.
  • Afternoon, Asakusa: ride the Ginza Line to the end of the line for pancakes at Coffee Tengoku and an unhurried loop of Senso-ji.

All three areas are on Tokyo Metro or Toei lines, so a 24-hour Tokyo Subway Ticket, bookable on Klook, covers the whole day. Finding more

This list is a starting point, not a ranking. Tabelog, Japan’s biggest restaurant-review site, publishes an annual “Kissaten 100” list; the 2026 edition, announced on 23 June 2026, includes 58 kissaten in Tokyo. It is in Japanese, but the names and maps are easy to follow. And the best kissaten are often the ones you simply walk past: a faded sign, a curtain in the doorway, a menu card in the window. Go in. Order the blend. Stay a while.

Frequently asked questions

What is a kissaten?

A kissaten (喫茶店, literally a tea-drinking shop) is Japan's old-style coffee house: table service, coffee brewed to order by siphon or hand drip, a short Showa-era menu of toast, Napolitan spaghetti, pudding and cream soda, and an interior that has kept its original character. The term jun-kissa, or pure kissaten, dates from the late 1920s, when coffee houses used it to set themselves apart from cafés with hostesses and alcohol.

What is the oldest kissaten in Tokyo?

Café Paulista in Ginza, which opened in 1911 and is Japan's oldest surviving kissaten. It has been at its current address on Chuo-dori in Ginza 8-chome since 1970. Japan's first Western-style coffee house, Kahiichakan, opened in Ueno in 1888 but closed within a few years.

Do kissaten in Tokyo take cards?

Many older kissaten are cash only, so carry a few ¥1,000 notes. Most also expect one order per person, since the rooms are small and seats are the business.

Can you smoke in Tokyo kissaten?

Mostly not. Tokyo's 2020 ordinance bans smoking in restaurants and cafés with staff. A few kissaten still allow it, usually because they sell tobacco at the counter and are registered as smoking-purpose shops, or because they have no employees. They must post a sign at the entrance, so check it before you sit down.

What should I order at a kissaten?

The house blend first, since it is the shop's signature. After that, whatever the place is known for: Vienna coffee at Ladrio, strawberry juice at Sabouru, aged coffee at Café de l'Ambre, French toast at Café Aaliya, or pancakes at Coffee Tengoku. Morning sets of toast, egg and coffee are good value until late morning.

How can I find more kissaten in Tokyo?

Tabelog publishes an annual Kissaten 100 list; the 2026 edition, announced on 23 June 2026, includes 58 kissaten in Tokyo. Jimbocho, Ginza and the old neighbourhoods of Asakusa and Ueno are the best areas to wander for them.
