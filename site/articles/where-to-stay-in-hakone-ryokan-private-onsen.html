A luxury night in Hakone is priced in three parts: the room, the meal plan and a small local charge that most guides skip. Hakone adds a bathing tax of ¥150 per person per night on hot-spring stays, and the word “private” on a booking page can mean three different baths. This guide sorts out both, then names ten places where a private soak is part of the stay.

It covers four areas (Gora, Sengokuhara and Kowakidani, Miyanoshita and Tonosawa, and Lake Ashi) and ten ryokan and hotels, with the bath each one actually publishes. The properties are researched picks, chosen for location, what each publishes and consistent guest feedback, and each was checked as operating in November 2026. Three kinds of “private” bath

Ryokan use the same word for three different things, and the difference decides what you are paying for.

  • An in-room bath (rotenburo or room onsen). A tub of hot-spring water on your own terrace or balcony, yours for the whole stay. This is what “private onsen” usually means, and it is what most of our picks offer. Some rooms have an indoor tub only, so look for the words “open-air”.
  • A reservable private bath (kashikiri). A shared bath house that you book for a slot, often 45 to 60 minutes, for you alone. Rules differ: some inns let you book ahead, others only on arrival.
  • A shared bath. The large men’s and women’s baths. They are the traditional experience, but they are not private, and some set rules about visible tattoos.

In the table at the end, “own bath” means an in-room bath that the property itself describes. Where a property’s own site does not say, we say so rather than guess.

The bathing tax and what else changes the bill

Hakone Town collects the bathing tax (nyuto-zei) from guests at hot-spring facilities; the inn adds it to your bill and pays it to the town. These are the rates on the town’s own page.

Who Rate Notes
Overnight guest ¥150 per person per night Added by the inn, usually at check-out
Day visitor ¥50 per person Day-use of a bath without staying
Exempt Children under 12; people using public or community baths; school groups led by teachers (overseas school trips excepted); people needing long-term treatment for illness Applies to both overnight and day guests

Our arithmetic: two adults staying two nights pay 2 × 2 × ¥150 = ¥600 in bathing tax, on top of the room and meal plan. A child under 12 adds nothing.

Hakone is also planning a separate lodging tax, which the town is aiming to start in April 2028. It is still at the draft-ordinance stage on the town’s site, so it does not apply to a 2026 or 2027 stay; check the town’s pages before booking for 2028.

Two other things move a ryokan bill more than the tax: the meal plan (most ryokan sell a stay with kaiseki dinner and breakfast, and some sell room-only) and the room type. We do not quote nightly rates here because they change by the hour; the booking page shows the plan, the room and the tax line.

Getting there

From Shinjuku, the Odakyu Romancecar reaches Hakone-Yumoto in about 80 minutes. The limited-express fee is ¥1,200 one way for adults, on top of the regular fare, and seats can be reserved from 10 a.m. Japan time one month before departure. You also need a regular ticket or an IC card to pass the gate.

The Hakone Freepass covers eight kinds of transport, including the mountain railway, cable car, ropeway, sightseeing cruise and Hakone Tozan buses in its area. From Shinjuku it costs ¥7,100 for two days or ¥7,500 for three, and from Odawara ¥6,000 or ¥6,400. Fukuzumiro is a five-minute drive from Hakone-Yumoto Station and Hotel Indigo is about 40 minutes by hotel shuttle from Odawara; ask each property about transfers when you book. For the lake, cable car and ropeway loop in a day, see where to book a Hakone day tour.

    Sengokuhara and Kowakidani: the quiet highlands

    Sengokuhara is the high plateau north of the lake, reached by Hakone Tozan bus from Hakone-Yumoto or by the Odakyu highway bus from Shinjuku, and Kowakidani sits between Gora and Miyanoshita. Both are about privacy: this is where the properties with a bath in every room cluster.

    Where to stay in Sengokuhara and Kowakidani

    • Kinnotake Sengokuhara, splurge. Nine uniquely designed room types, each with an open-air onsen. Kyo kaiseki is served in the privacy of your room, and the inn says its rooms are designed for guests aged 12 and over. Choose it for a quiet stay for two. Watch out: the page we read gives no check-in times or tattoo policy. Check rates on Booking.com →
    • Hakone Retreat före, splurge. A forest property in Sengokuhara whose “villa 1/f” is a set of detached villas with a hot spring each; the site describes log cabins with a wood-burning stove and an open-air or semi-open-air bath. Choose it for a house of your own rather than a corridor of rooms. Watch out: it posted a notice in October 2026 that the public bath was temporarily closed for maintenance, and reception hours are 10:00 to 18:00. Check rates on Booking.com →
    • Hakone Suishoen, splurge. In Kowakidani, with 23 individually designed rooms; the inn says all of them have an open-air bath with free-flowing natural hot-spring water. Choose it for a smaller ryokan with a bath in every room and a teppan-and-Japanese restaurant on site. Watch out: its page does not publish check-in times, meal plans or a tattoo policy. Check rates on Booking.com →
    • Hakone Kowakien Ten-yu, splurge. Near the Hakone Open-air Museum, with every room carrying a private open-air hot-spring bath, in Superior, Maisonette and Executive Suite types. The site puts it about 90 minutes from Shinjuku, a six-minute bus ride from the museum and Gora Park, and a 13-minute walk from Chisuji Falls. Choose it for private baths at a larger scale.     Miyanoshita and Tonosawa: the old Hakone

    Miyanoshita and Tonosawa are the old hot-spring villages on the Hayakawa river, built before the cable car. Staying here is about buildings and age more than about a bath in the room.

    Where to stay in Miyanoshita and Tonosawa

    • Fukuzumiro, splurge. A riverside inn founded in 1890 whose building is registered as a cultural property of Japan, with 17 rooms and kaiseki served in the room. Its private baths are taken in order of arrival from 15:00; the inn does not accept advance reservations for them. The inn’s FAQ says there are no particular restrictions on tattoos at the hotel, with dinner from 18:00 and check-in from 15:00. It is a five-minute drive from Hakone-Yumoto Station. Choose it for history and a calm riverside evening. Watch out: the page we read does not say whether any room has its own bath. Check rates on Booking.com →
    • Fujiya Hotel, splurge. Established in 1878 and one of Japan’s classic resort hotels, in Miyanoshita. Choose it for the building and the setting rather than for a bath in the room; the site speaks of Miyanoshita hot-spring water but does not describe in-room outdoor baths. Watch out: it is a Western-style hotel, and the site carries a notice of a partial closure of the spa’s indoor men’s bath. Check rates on Booking.com →

    Lake Ashi: the water and Mount Fuji

    The lake fills part of the old caldera. Evenings are quiet once the last boats and buses have gone, and on a clear day Mount Fuji stands beyond the hills. Both hotels below trade a bath in every room for water views.

    Where to stay on Lake Ashi

    • Hakone Ashinoko Hanaori, comfortable. At Togendai, the ropeway’s lake terminus. Some room types have an open-air bath on the terrace (a ceramic tub in the standard room), and the hotel has two semi-open-air private baths for overnight guests. Many plans include two meals, served as Japanese and Western buffets. Choose it for a lake-end base with a private bath at a gentler price level. Watch out: it closes entirely after check-out on 12 January 2027 and reopens for check-in on 22 January. Check rates on Booking.com →
    • Odakyu Hotel de Yama, comfortable. On the shore at Moto-Hakone, with a 130,000 m² garden by the lake and Mount Fuji as its backdrop. The hotel’s hot-spring rooms have a bath, and the open-air bath is in the shared bath. Choose it for the lake and the garden. Watch out: it does not publish an in-room open-air bath, so it does not give the private soak that is the point of this guide; pick it for the view. Check rates on Booking.com →

    How to book a luxury stay in Hakone

    • Read the bath line, not the room name. “Hot-spring room” can mean an indoor tub. Look for “open-air”, and for whether the bath is on a terrace or in a shared house.
    • Decide on meals first. A ryokan evening runs on a fixed dinner time, often 18:00 or 19:00, and arriving late can change the meal. Choose a plan with dinner for the full ryokan experience.
    • Count the tax. Add ¥150 per adult per night to the total, as in the worked example above.
    • Watch the season. Autumn colour and weekends fill the small inns first, and several of these properties have January 2027 closures for maintenance.
    • Check the room’s size, the cancellation line and the age rule (Kinnotake takes guests aged 12 and over). For how the booking sites compare, see hotel booking sites compared.

    Frequently asked questions

    Which Hakone ryokan have a private open-air bath in every room?

    Among our picks, the properties say so for every room at Kinnotake Sengokuhara, Hakone Kowakien Ten-yu and Hakone Suishoen. Tensui Saryo has an open-air bath on each room's terrace, and Hotel Indigo Hakone Gora states that all rooms have a private onsen, though it does not say which are outdoors.

    What is the difference between an in-room bath and a private bath you reserve?

    An in-room bath is a hot-spring tub on your own terrace or balcony, yours for the whole stay. A reservable private bath (kashikiri) is a bath house you book for a slot. A shared bath is the large men's and women's bath, which is not private.

    What is Hakone's bathing tax?

    Hakone Town charges ¥150 per person per night for overnight guests at hot-spring facilities and ¥50 for day visitors, added by the inn. Children under 12, users of public or community baths, and teacher-led school groups (overseas school trips excepted) are exempt. Two adults for two nights pay ¥600.

    Is Hakone introducing a lodging tax?

    The town is aiming to introduce a lodging tax from April 2028, but on its own site the ordinance is still a draft outline, so it does not apply to a 2026 or 2027 stay.

    How do I get to Hakone from Shinjuku?

    The Odakyu Romancecar takes about 80 minutes to Hakone-Yumoto, with a limited-express fee of ¥1,200 one way on top of the regular fare. The Hakone Freepass costs ¥7,100 for two days or ¥7,500 for three from Shinjuku.

    Do these ryokan allow tattoos?

    Policies differ and most of our picks do not publish one on the pages we read. Fukuzumiro's FAQ says there are no particular restrictions on tattoos at the hotel, though other facilities you bathe at may have their own rules. An in-room bath avoids the question.
