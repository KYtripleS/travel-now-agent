Solo Travel Safety Rules: 10 Practical Habits for Safer Trips

By Martin, Gently Yonder writer

Ten calm solo travel safety habits: share your itinerary, use STEP or FCDO alerts, safe airport transport, split money, offline maps, emergency numbers, scams.

Last updated 1 Nov 2026 · 13 min read

Most solo trips pass without incident, and the habits that help are small and dull: tell someone where you will be, have your first night booked, get from the airport in a transport you trust, keep your money in more than one place, and know the number to call. This guide puts ten of them in order, from the week before you leave to your last night out. The tone is calm on purpose. The aim is not to make a trip feel dangerous but to take the avoidable problems off the table, using what the UK Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office (FCDO), the US State Department’s STEP programme and other official sources say.

The ten habits

1. Share an itinerary, and agree how often you will check in

Leave your plans with someone at home: where you are sleeping each night, your flight and train details, and roughly when you will message. The FCDO’s solo travel guidance says to leave an itinerary with someone at home and agree how regularly you will be in contact, and suggests leaving photocopies of important documents such as your passport and insurance policy. Agree what happens if you miss a check-in, so your contact does not have to guess. Keep the plan loose enough that a changed itinerary means a quick message rather than a worry.

2. Register with STEP (US) or follow FCDO travel advice (UK)

If you are a US citizen, the Smart Traveler Enrollment Program (STEP) is a free service that lets US citizens travelling or living abroad receive the latest security updates from the nearest US embassy or consulate. The information you give also lets the embassy contact you in an emergency, and it can be used to try to reach you if family at home cannot. Enrolment is on the State Department’s STEP site.

If you are travelling from the UK, the FCDO publishes country-by-country travel advice and says you should sign up for email alerts so you hear when it changes. Use that advice alongside your own research. Nationals of other countries can look for the same service from their own foreign ministry. Either way, read the destination’s advice before you book, because it can change what you plan to do.

3. Arrive in daylight, with the first night booked

Arrival is the point of a solo trip where tiredness and an unfamiliar place coincide. The FCDO says to book accommodation before you travel, in particular your first night, because you are most vulnerable when you first arrive in a foreign country. We would add a simple filter of our own: when choosing flights and trains, prefer ones that land in daylight, and keep the evening arrival for a place you already know. If a delay pushes you into the dark, you already have a bed to go to. Have a backup too. The FCDO suggests knowing the address of where you are staying, in case your host or transfer does not turn up, and knowing what other accommodation is available.

4. Use verified transport from the airport

Decide how you will get from the airport to your bed before you land. The FCDO suggests arranging airport transfers before you arrive and using only official, licensed and reputable taxis, and says to travel in busy train carriages and avoid hitchhiking. In practice that means an official taxi rank or a transfer booked in advance, a ride you order yourself through an app, or public transport you have checked. Be wary of anyone who approaches you inside the arrivals hall offering a ride.

Two examples from the FCDO’s country pages show the pattern. Its advice for Chile warns that unlicensed taxi drivers at Santiago airport add digits to the fare, quoting 5,000,000 Chilean pesos instead of 50,000, and its advice for South Africa warns of unregulated drivers posing as app-based taxis, especially at airports. The fix is the same everywhere: settle the price or use the meter before you get in, and use only a vehicle you can identify.

5. Check the accommodation: reviews, location and the door

Research before you book. The FCDO says to research accommodation options carefully, including checking the location, and to have alternative accommodation as back-up. Read recent reviews for mentions of the neighbourhood after dark, noise, and how the staff handle problems. Note how far you will walk from transport at night.

Once you are inside, the FCDO’s solo travel guidance lists habits that cost nothing:

  • never leave your key where someone can note your room number
  • close your windows, especially on the ground floor or if there is a balcony
  • lock your door when you are inside the room
  • use the spy hole or chain before opening the door, especially if you are not expecting anyone
  • consider a door wedge to place behind the door

It also asks you to check that the accommodation has smoke alarms and fire exits, and to be aware of carbon monoxide risks from badly fitted water heaters or cookers. If you are staying in a dormitory, our guide to keeping valuables safe in hostels covers lockers and bags.

6. Split your money across places

The aim is that no single loss, whether a stolen bag or a card swallowed by a machine, leaves you stranded. Our version of the rule is: carry one card and a small amount of cash for the day, keep a second card and a reserve of cash in a different place (the hotel room, or a different bag), and know how you would get money in an emergency. The FCDO’s solo guidance says to make sure you can access your money while abroad, including quickly in an emergency, and its checklist says to ensure you can reach enough money if your main payment method fails, for example if you lose your phone and cannot complete two-factor authentication.

The FCDO’s advice for France adds the same idea for pickpockets: keep your passport, credit cards and other valuables in separate bags or pockets, use the inside compartments of bags, carry your bag across your body rather than on your shoulder, and do not get distracted around tourist attractions and ATMs. Its advice for Thailand says not to lose sight of your card during transactions, and to use an ATM in a bank and protect your PIN. A slim passport and card organiser keeps documents in one place; it is a convenience, not a security measure, and it should not hold everything you own.

7. Carry a phone with data and offline maps

A working phone is the single most useful safety tool you carry, which is why the FCDO’s checklist asks for a backup plan for key information, such as accommodation and flight details, passport details, contact numbers and your insurance policy, in case you lose your phone. Store it somewhere other than the phone: printed, or in secure online storage shared with someone you trust.

8. Know the emergency numbers

Write these down and save them in your phone before you go. They differ by country, and the numbers below come from official sources:

Where Number Source note
European Union 112 Free from fixed and mobile phones everywhere in the EU; connects to police, ambulance or fire brigade. Some countries also keep national numbers
United States 911 The US Federal Communications Commission describes 911 as the universal emergency number for telephone services in the US
United Kingdom 999 The NHS says 999 is for life-threatening emergencies
Japan 110 police, 119 fire and ambulance The Japan National Tourism Organization lists these; it also runs a 24-hour multilingual Japan Visitor Hotline on 050-3816-2787

Elsewhere, look up the local number in the FCDO or State Department country page for your destination, and note the contact details of your nearest embassy or consulate. The FCDO’s checklist says British travellers can contact the nearest British embassy, high commission or consulate, or the FCDO in London, 24 hours a day on 020 7008 5000. If you are fearful for your safety or in danger, the FCDO says to contact the local police.

9. Drink safety

Alcohol is a factor in many travel injuries, and spiking is a recognised risk. UK government travel guidance gives practical points that apply in any country:

  • keep an eye on your drink and do not leave it unattended; if it smells or tastes odd, do not drink it
  • do not accept drinks from strangers
  • buy your own drinks
  • be aware that alcohol can hit harder abroad because measures are sometimes larger
  • plan your trip home before you go out, note the address of where you are staying, and stay in contact with friends

The same guidance warns about methanol poisoning from counterfeit or contaminated spirits and cocktails in some parts of the world, and the UK’s travel-health service (TravelHealthPro) says informal and illegal production of fake alcohol is common in many parts of the world and that excessive alcohol use may invalidate insurance claims. If you feel more drunk than you should, drowsy or confused, get to safety and seek emergency medical help; for suspected spiking the guidance says to report it to the local police.

10. Scams: learn the pattern, not the list

Scams change by city but follow a few patterns, and spotting the pattern is more useful than memorising a list:

  • The helpful stranger. Someone distracts you while a second person takes your bag. The FCDO describes this for France, where pickpockets can work in gangs, with one distracting you while another goes into your bag, and for Chile.
  • The official who is not. The FCDO’s advice for Spain warns of thieves posing as police officers who ask to see your wallet, noting that genuine officers will ask for ID but not for wallets or purses.
  • The price that moves. Taxi fares quoted one way and charged another, as in the Santiago example above. Agree the fare or insist on the meter.
  • The payment you cannot read. The FCDO’s advice for Chile also describes people persuaded to enter a PIN into a card reader whose display cannot be read, and its Thailand advice mentions ATM skimming.
  • The offer that is too good. The FCDO warns of job scams in Thailand that are too good to be true.

The common thread is pressure. A calm refusal, and a step away, is enough. In the FCDO’s words, do not be afraid to draw attention to yourself by shouting and making a fuss, and remember that being safe is more important than being polite.

If something goes wrong

Get to a safe place first and contact the local police if you are fearful for your safety or in danger. For anything that needs insurance, keep copies of reports and receipts. The FCDO has advice for British nationals who are victims of crime abroad, and US citizens can contact the nearest embassy or consulate. If your bag does not arrive or is stolen in transit, work through our lost luggage first-48-hours guide. If the bag is yours to track, an Apple AirTag in the case lets you see where it is, and clear luggage tags with a privacy flap carry your contact details; our tracker comparison explains the options.

Travel insurance

The FCDO says to have appropriate travel insurance even if you are visiting friends and family or know the country well, and its checklist says to check that a policy covers the places you will visit, how long you will be away and any planned activities. TravelHealthPro adds that the cover should include planned activities, pre-existing health problems, belongings and medical repatriation. Read the exclusions around alcohol before you rely on a claim. Our best travel insurance 2026 guide compares policies.

Frequently asked questions

What are the most important solo travel safety habits?

Share your itinerary and agree how often you will check in, book your first night before you travel, arrange official or pre-booked transport from the airport, keep money and cards in more than one place, carry a phone with data and offline maps, know the local emergency number, keep your drink in sight, and learn the common scam patterns. The UK Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office (FCDO) lists several of these in its solo and independent travel guidance.

What is STEP and who can use it?

The Smart Traveler Enrollment Program (STEP) is a free US State Department service for US citizens travelling or living abroad. It sends security updates from the nearest US embassy or consulate and lets the embassy use your details to contact you in an emergency. UK travellers can sign up for FCDO email alerts for their destination instead.

What are the emergency numbers for the EU, US, UK and Japan?

112 in the European Union (free from fixed and mobile phones everywhere in the EU), 911 in the United States, 999 in the United Kingdom, and 110 for police and 119 for fire and ambulance in Japan. Check the official page for any other destination, and note your nearest embassy or consulate.

Is it safer to arrive in daylight when travelling alone?

It is our own preference rather than an official rule, but it fits the FCDO's advice: it says you are most vulnerable when you first arrive in a foreign country, tired and unsure of your surroundings, and recommends booking your first night's accommodation in advance. Choosing a daylight arrival and knowing exactly where you are going reduces the number of decisions you make while tired.

How do I avoid drink spiking when travelling alone?

UK government travel guidance says to keep an eye on your drink and not leave it unattended, not to drink it if it smells or tastes odd, and not to accept drinks from strangers. Plan your journey home before you go out and note the address of where you are staying. If you feel more drunk than expected, drowsy or confused, get to safety, seek emergency medical help if needed and report it to the local police.

How can I recognise a travel scam?

Look for the pattern rather than the detail: someone distracting you while another person takes your bag; a person posing as police who asks to see your wallet (the FCDO says genuine officers will ask for ID, not wallets); fares that change after you start the journey; payment machines you cannot read; and offers that sound too good to be true. A calm refusal and a step away is usually enough.
