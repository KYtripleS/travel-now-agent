Tokyo’s cherry trees open, on average, on 24 March and reach full bloom on 31 March, by the Japan Meteorological Agency’s normals for 1991 to 2020; the sample tree it watches stands in the grounds of Yasukuni Shrine, a short walk from Chidorigafuchi. In 2025 the trees opened on 24 March and were full on 30 March; in 2026 they opened on 19 March and were full by 28 March. Those are the weeks everyone wants, so it pays to hold a refundable room early, and in 2027 there is a second reason to book with care: Tokyo’s accommodation tax changes on 1 April.

When to go: the dates to aim for

By the Japan Meteorological Agency’s count for Tokyo:

Year First flowers Full bloom
Normal (1991–2020) 24 March 31 March
2025 24 March 30 March
2026 19 March 28 March

The agency calls the trees open when five or six flowers are out on the sample tree, and in full bloom when about 80% of its flowers are open. Full bloom has come six to nine days after the first flowers, and the blossom does not hold for long after that. Aim for the last ten days of March, and stay four or five nights rather than two so that an early or late year still overlaps with your stay.

The first forecasts come from Japan’s private weather companies: for the 2026 season, the Japan Meteorological Corporation published on 18 December 2025, Weathernews on 8 January and the Japan Weather Association, which runs tenki.jp, on 29 January, each updating as spring approached.

Asakusa and the Sumida River: blossom on both banks

Asakusa sits on the Sumida River. Sumida Park’s cherry trees line both banks for about a kilometre between Azuma Bridge and Sakura Bridge, with Tokyo Skytree across the water; the avenue is said to go back to the eighth shogun, Tokugawa Yoshimune, who had cherries planted so that ordinary people could enjoy them too. Senso-ji’s grounds have cherry trees of their own, and Ueno is three stops away on the Ginza line. Stay here and you can be under the trees within minutes of leaving the hotel, early in the morning before the crowds.

Ueno and Yanaka: the classic park picnic

Ueno Park, one of the five places designated as Japan’s first public parks in 1873, has some 800 cherry trees. It has been known for them since the seventeenth century, when the priest Tenkai, who founded the temple of Kan’ei-ji here, had cherries brought from Mount Yoshino; the 2026 Ueno cherry festival ran from 14 March to 5 April. It is the Tokyo picnic in its most crowded and cheerful form. Yanaka, a short walk to the north-west, is the quiet counterpart, with a cherry-lined avenue, known as Sakura-dori, through its old cemetery. The Skyliner from Narita arrives at Keisei Ueno, next to Ueno Station.

Marunouchi and Hibiya: the palace moat and the boats

Chidorigafuchi, on the north-west side of the Imperial Palace, has about 260 cherry trees along a 700-metre path beside the moat, and Chiyoda City runs a boat pier on the water below them, with rowing boats, pedal boats and swan boats; in cherry season a boat costs ¥1,500 for 30 minutes. From Otemachi, by Tokyo Station, it is two stops on the Tozai line to Kudanshita. Marunouchi and Hibiya are the polished, central choice: the Shinkansen and the Narita Express at Tokyo Station, and Hibiya Park across the road from the Peninsula.

Shibuya: the Meguro River

The Meguro River has some 800 cherry trees along about 3.8 kilometres of both banks, lit up at night in season. Nakameguro Station, two stops from Shibuya on the Tokyu Toyoko line (one on an express), is a short walk from the river. There is no lawn for a picnic here, just a slow walk under the branches past the cafés along the banks. Shibuya itself has the trains and the energy, and Yoyogi Park is a walk to the north.

Shinjuku: Shinjuku Gyoen

Shinjuku Gyoen, a national garden run by the Ministry of the Environment, has some 900 cherry trees of about 70 varieties, from early kinds at their best in February to double-flowered ones in mid-April, so there is blossom here before and after the main peak. Entry for adults is ¥500, and alcohol is not allowed. By the garden’s own directions, the Shinjuku Gate is a ten-minute walk from the South Exit of Shinjuku Station, and five minutes from Shinjuku-gyoemmae Station on the Marunouchi line.

How to book for a season that moves, and a tax that changes

  • Book a refundable rate now for the nights you want, and adjust when the forecasts firm up in January and February; check each rate’s cancellation deadline.
  • Know the tax date. Until 31 March 2027, Tokyo’s accommodation tax is ¥100 a person a night on rooms of ¥10,000 to ¥14,999 a person, ¥200 from ¥15,000, and nothing below ¥10,000. From 1 April 2027 it becomes 3% of the room charge on rooms of ¥13,000 or more a person a night, with private lodging included. On a ¥40,000-a-person night, that is ¥1,200 instead of ¥200. Late-March nights come before the change; if your stay runs into April, ask the hotel how those nights will be charged. See Japan’s tourist taxes in 2026 for the rest of the country.
  • Stay longer, not later. Four or five nights across the last ten days of March give you better odds than two perfect ones.
  • If Tokyo is full, look at the stations one or two stops out on the same lines; our full guide to where to stay in Tokyo has more hotels by area, and hotel booking sites compared goes through where to book.

Getting around in blossom season

  • Take the subway, not the bus or a taxi: the Ginza line links Asakusa and Ueno, the Tozai line runs from Otemachi to Kudanshita for Chidorigafuchi, and the Tokyu Toyoko line runs from Shibuya to Nakameguro.
  • Go early: Ueno Park opens at 5 a.m. and the Meguro River path is a public street, so you can walk both at first light, before the day’s crowds.

Frequently asked questions

When will the cherry blossoms bloom in Tokyo in 2027?

No one knows yet. The Japan Meteorological Agency's normals for Tokyo are first flowers on 24 March and full bloom on 31 March; in 2026 the trees opened on 19 March and were full on 28 March. For the 2026 season the first forecasts came out between 18 December and 29 January, so expect the 2027 ones on a similar timetable.

What is the best area to stay in Tokyo for cherry blossom?

Asakusa puts Sumida Park's riverside cherries and Senso-ji on foot, with Ueno Park three stops away on the Ginza line. Ueno suits the classic park picnic, Marunouchi the Chidorigafuchi moat, Shibuya the Meguro River and Shinjuku the Shinjuku Gyoen garden.

How far ahead should I book a Tokyo hotel for cherry blossom season?

As soon as your dates are firm. Book a refundable rate across the last ten days of March, then adjust when the forecasts firm up in January and February, checking each rate's cancellation deadline.

How long do the cherry blossoms last in Tokyo?

Full bloom has come six to nine days after the first flowers in recent years, and the blossom does not hold for long after that. Shinjuku Gyoen's 70 or so varieties stretch the season, from early kinds in February to double-flowered ones in mid-April.

Does Tokyo's accommodation tax change in 2027?

Yes. Until 31 March 2027 it is ¥100 a person a night on rooms of ¥10,000 to ¥14,999 a person and ¥200 from ¥15,000, with cheaper rooms exempt. From 1 April 2027 it becomes 3% of the room charge on rooms of ¥13,000 or more a person a night, with private lodging included.

Where is Tokyo's official cherry blossom tree?

The Japan Meteorological Agency has watched a sample tree in the grounds of Yasukuni Shrine, near Chidorigafuchi, since 1966. It calls Tokyo's cherries open when five or six flowers are out on that tree, and in full bloom when about 80% of its flowers are open.
