Hotels around Indre By, with prices

The map shows the hotels and apartments around Indre By that booking sites such as Booking.com and Expedia list, with nightly prices; our own picks follow further down. It comes from our partner Stay22, which pays us a commission if you book through it.

Copenhagen is one of the few big European cities that adds no tourist tax to the hotel bill. Denmark has none, and in 2026 Copenhagen still has no legal power to charge one. What is in the bill is Danish VAT at 25%, and it is already inside the price you are shown. So the number on the screen is close to the number you pay, and that changes how you compare hotels here with Amsterdam or Berlin. Where you stay decides the rest: how much of the city you see on foot, and how quickly you get to the airport.

This guide covers five areas, from the old centre to the Lakes, 12 hotels, the airport links and what the bill really includes. The hotels are researched picks, chosen for location, what each property publishes and consistent guest feedback, and each was checked as operating in October 2026. No hotel pays to appear here.

Copenhagen’s hotel tax: none, but 25% VAT

A Danish municipality cannot simply decide to charge visitors per night. When Copenhagen’s City Council discussed a tourism levy in June 2024, its own papers set out the obstacle: a tourism levy requires legal authority, and Parliament would have to give municipalities the power to collect one. The council voted to ask the government for that power; it has not been granted. In October 2026 a hotel charge for Copenhagen and Aarhus was reported to be under discussion for the 2027 budget, but Dansk Erhverv, the Danish Chamber of Commerce, reported that it was not in the government’s finance bill. For 2026, there is no tourist tax.

Charge Rate in Copenhagen, 2026
Tourist tax, per person and per night None
VAT (moms) on the room 25%
VAT as a share of the price you see 20%

Denmark’s Tax Agency puts the VAT rate at generally 25% of the value of goods and services, and hotel rooms carry that full rate; Spain, by comparison, charges hotels a reduced 10%. Because prices for consumers include VAT, the agency also notes that VAT is 20% of a retail price. When you compare a Copenhagen rate with one from a city that adds its tax on top, remember that the Danish figure is already the total.

Our arithmetic: Andersen Boutique Hotel lists its Cool rooms from DKK 1,650 a night. Of that, DKK 330 is VAT, and four nights cost DKK 6,600, about €885 at the krone’s fixed central rate of DKK 7.46 to the euro (approximate). The same stay in Berlin would also carry its 7.5% City Tax. For other cities, see hotel taxes around the world.

Getting in from the airport, and around

At Copenhagen Airport, the metro and the train both leave from Terminal 3.

  • The M2 metro runs from the airport station, built onto the end of Terminal 3, to Christianshavn, Kongens Nytorv and Nørreport. The Metro puts the ride to the centre at 14 minutes. It runs through the night: about every 2 to 3 minutes by day, and from 1am to 7am about every 8 minutes after Friday and Saturday and about every 20 minutes on other nights.
  • The fare is a 3-zone ticket, DKK 30, valid for 1 hour 30 minutes on the metro, buses and trains; the Metro notes that it covers the trip between the city centre and the airport. A 2-zone ticket is DKK 24. An adult can take two children under 12 free, and 12- to 15-year-olds pay half. The airport’s metro ticket machines take coins and cards but not notes.
  • The train runs from Terminal 3, tracks 1 and 2, to Copenhagen Central Station (København H). Tickets are sold at the DSB office and machines in Terminal 3 and in the DSB app; you cannot buy one on board, and travelling without one risks a fine.
  • For a few days, the City Pass Small covers zones 1 to 4, which include the whole metro and the airport: DKK 100 for 24 hours, DKK 160 for 48, DKK 220 for 72, DKK 280 for 96 and DKK 340 for 120. City Pass Large, for zones 1 to 99, costs DKK 200 to DKK 680.
  • Buying tickets: the Rejsebillet app sells single tickets and the City Pass and has replaced the old DOT Tickets app; machines at metro and train stations also sell them. The Rejsekort app works by checking in and out, and takes 20% off journeys that begin outside rush hour.
  • The Copenhagen Card DISCOVER, from its official site, includes public transport in zones 1 to 99, the airport trip and entry to more than 80 attractions: DKK 589 for 24 hours, DKK 859 for 48, DKK 1,039 for 72, DKK 1,219 for 96 and DKK 1,419 for 120. It pays only if you will visit several paid attractions each day.

Indre By and Strøget: the first-trip base

Indre By, the inner city, is where most first trips start. Strøget, Copenhagen’s main pedestrian shopping street, runs through it towards Kongens Nytorv, with the Latin Quarter and the Round Tower, a 17th-century observatory with a spiral ramp to the top, just off it. Torvehallerne, the market halls by Nørreport Station, are the practical lunch stop. Kongens Nytorv and Nørreport are both on the M2, so the airport is one ride away; Nørreport also has the S-trains.

Nyhavn: the painted quay

Nyhavn, the “new harbour”, was completed in 1673 for the city’s sea trade. Today it is the colourful waterfront of townhouses and old wooden ships that most visitors come to see, and Visit Copenhagen’s advice is to see it and then explore the streets around it. Behind the quay are Kongens Nytorv, the Royal Theatre’s old stage, the Amalienborg palaces and, across the water, the Royal Danish Opera. The quay is crowded by day and lively at night; the side streets are quieter.

Where to stay in Nyhavn

  • 71 Nyhavn Hotel, comfortable. In a Nyhavn warehouse completed in 1805, with 130 rooms under Pomeranian pine beams and breakfast in the old warehouse cellar. Choose it for the harbour from your window. Watch out: the view is sold as its own room categories, Deluxe View and Partial View, so check which one you are booking. Check rates on Booking.com →
  • Hotel Sanders, splurge. Just behind the Nyhavn canal, with 54 rooms, each designed differently, Sanders Kitchen, the TATA cocktail bar, a rooftop and a courtyard, and a complimentary minibar. The single rooms take their design from old luxury train cabins. Choose it for a small, carefully designed hotel by the water. Watch out: the Double Rooms are 12 to 13 m² and face a green atrium, so they get less daylight. Check rates on Booking.com →

Christianshavn: canals and houseboats

Christianshavn, across the harbour from the centre, was established in the early 17th century, and it is still a district of canals, old houses, houseboats and streets running between the waterfront and the old fortifications. The Church of Our Saviour is its landmark, and the green paths on the Rampart of Christianshavn lead on towards Christiania. The evenings are quieter than in Nyhavn, and Christianshavn station is on the M2: the airport is direct, and Kongens Nytorv one stop away.

Where to stay in Christianshavn

  • NH Collection Copenhagen, comfortable. At Strandgade 7, in the 1950s headquarters of the B&W shipyards, designed by the modernist architect Palle Suenson and now a listed building, with 394 rooms, a gym, rental bikes and a rooftop bar from April to October. Choose it for mid-century design on the harbour. Watch out: it is a large hotel, and only “many” rooms have harbour views; book a view category if that is the point. Check rates on Booking.com →
  • CPH Living, good value. A floating hotel on a converted barge at Langebrogade, open since 2009 and, by its own account, Denmark’s first, with 12 double rooms, each with a glass wall onto the harbour, a rooftop terrace for guests and a free basic breakfast. Choose it for the harbour view at a lower price. Watch out: with 12 rooms there is little choice, and arrivals after 6pm must contact the hotel first. Check rates on Booking.com →

Vesterbro: Kødbyen and the station

Vesterbro, west of the centre, was once the city’s red-light district. Visit Copenhagen now describes it as a mix of family living, nightlife, fashion boutiques and some of its old grit, and Istedgade is its best-known street. Kødbyen, the former meatpacking district, is where the evenings start: its industrial buildings hold restaurants, bars, galleries and clubs, and parts of the meat trade still work alongside them. Copenhagen Central Station and Tivoli Gardens are on its eastern edge, so the train to the airport is at hand.

Where to stay in Vesterbro

  • Villa Copenhagen, splurge. At Tietgensgade 35-39, in the former Central Post & Telegraph Head Office, dating from 1912, with a 25-metre heated rooftop pool kept at 28 to 30°C all year, a sauna, the Rug Kitchen and Rug Bakery, and rooms from 17 m². Choose it for the pool and the station next door. Watch out: the pool is for staying guests only, and children may use it only from 7am to 7pm. Check rates on Booking.com →
  • Andersen Boutique Hotel, comfortable. On Helgolandsgade in Vesterbro, with 69 rooms decorated with Designers Guild wallpaper and fabrics, and a free glass of wine in the lobby every day from 5 to 6pm. Book direct and its CONCEPT24 offer lets you keep the room for 24 hours. Choose it for character near Kødbyen and the station. Watch out: the Cool rooms are 11 to 13 m². Check rates on Booking.com →
  • Steel House Copenhagen, budget. On Herholdtsgade, by the Lakes, an “industrial chic” hostel with 4-bed and 6-bed dorms from DKK 145 a night, double, twin and single rooms (some with a terrace), a pool and a fitness room. Choose it for a hostel bed with hotel extras. Watch out: the pool and the fitness room cost extra. Check rates on Booking.com →

Nørrebro, Østerbro and the Lakes: local streets

The Lakes, a chain of long city lakes, divide the centre from the residential districts to the north and west. Nørrebro, beyond them, is the city’s most culturally diverse neighbourhood and known for its food; Jægersborggade is the compact place to start, and Assistens Cemetery, where Hans Christian Andersen and Søren Kierkegaard are buried, is also one of the district’s most used green spaces. Østerbro, to the north-east, is what Visit Copenhagen calls the elegant part of town, and one of its greenest, with Fælledparken, the Parken stadium and a walk by the water to Kastellet and the Little Mermaid. Both are for a second trip, or for anyone who would rather live in the city than look at it.

Where to stay by the Lakes

  • Hotel Kong Arthur, comfortable. On Nørre Søgade, facing the Lakes in the Nansensgade neighbourhood, with the Ni’mat Spa, three courtyards, three affiliated restaurants, a 24-hour reception and bar, and the same 5-to-6pm “cozy hour” as Ibsens Hotel. Choose it for the spa and the walk by the water. Watch out: the Double Economy rooms are 14 to 19 m² with a semi-double bed. Check rates on Booking.com →
  • Rye115, good value. A small family-run hotel at Ryesgade 115 in Østerbro, in a grand apartment from 1905, with 20 rooms furnished with vintage Nordic design, homemade organic buns at breakfast and tea and cake every afternoon. Choose it for a local base at a fair price. Watch out: there is no lift, and the triple and family rooms are on the second floor. Check rates on Booking.com →

How to book Copenhagen

  • Count the VAT, once. There is no tourist tax, and Danish prices include 25% VAT. If a booking site shows a lower rate “before taxes”, the total is a quarter more: a DKK 1,320 room before VAT is DKK 1,650 with it.
  • Check the room size. Some rooms here are small by design: Ibsens Hotel’s Tiny rooms are 10 to 12 m², Andersen’s Cool rooms 11 to 13 m² and Hotel Sanders’ Double Rooms 12 to 13 m². If you need space, book the category above the cheapest.
  • Mind the stairs and the late arrival. Rye115 has no lift, and CPH Living asks guests arriving after 6pm to contact it first.
  • Compare totals. Hotel booking sites compared goes through where to book.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best area to stay in Copenhagen for a first trip?

Indre By, the inner city: Strøget, the Round Tower, Kongens Nytorv and Nyhavn are on foot, and Kongens Nytorv and Nørreport are on the M2 metro to the airport. Choose Christianshavn for canals and houseboats, Vesterbro for Kødbyen's nightlife and the train station, and Nørrebro or Østerbro for local streets by the Lakes.

Does Copenhagen have a tourist tax?

No. In 2026 neither Denmark nor Copenhagen charges a tourist tax. A municipal tourism levy needs legal authority from Parliament, which Copenhagen asked for in 2024 but has not been given, and a hotel charge discussed for the 2027 budget was not in the government's finance bill.

How much VAT is on a hotel room in Denmark?

25%, Denmark's standard VAT rate, and it is already included in the price you are shown, so it makes up 20% of that price. A room listed at DKK 1,650 a night includes DKK 330 of VAT.

How do you get from Copenhagen Airport to the city centre?

Take the M2 metro from Terminal 3 to Christianshavn, Kongens Nytorv or Nørreport; the Metro puts the ride to the centre at 14 minutes and it runs through the night. A 3-zone ticket costs DKK 30. Trains also run from Terminal 3 to Copenhagen Central Station.

How much is a metro ticket in Copenhagen?

An adult single ticket costs DKK 24 for 2 zones and DKK 30 for 3 zones, which covers the airport trip and the whole metro. The City Pass Small, for zones 1 to 4 including the airport, costs DKK 100 for 24 hours and up to DKK 340 for 120 hours.

Is the Copenhagen Card worth it?

Only if you will visit several paid attractions each day. The Copenhagen Card DISCOVER costs DKK 589 for 24 hours and includes entry to more than 80 attractions and public transport in zones 1 to 99, including the airport; for transport alone, the City Pass Small costs DKK 100 for 24 hours.
