Europe Rail Passes in 2026: What Just Changed, and When a Pass Actually Wins

By Casey, Gently Yonder editor

Eurail and Interrail merged on 9 September 2026 into one range for everyone. What a pass costs in 2026, why compulsory seat reservations on French, Italian and Spanish fast trains decide whether it pays, and the passholder quota that means a valid pass is not a seat.

Two things you need before you buy anything.

First, the thing that changed this month. Eurail and Interrail — for years two names for nearly the same product, split by where you lived — merged on 9 September 2026 into a single range, called Interrail, available to everyone. If you researched this even a few weeks ago, the “which one am I allowed to buy?” question you were trying to answer has stopped existing. Because this is days old, check the current wording on the official site before you pay.

Second, the thing that decides whether a pass is worth it. A rail pass is not a ticket. On the trains most people actually want — the fast ones through France, Italy and Spain — you still have to buy a compulsory seat reservation on top, and the cheap passholder seats are rationed. That single fact is why so many people come home saying the pass wasn’t worth it.

What a pass actually is

Two shapes, and the choice between them matters more than the headline price:

  • Flexi — a set number of travel days inside a window. Five days within one month, say. You activate a day when you use it.
  • Continuous — unlimited travel every day for a fixed stretch.

Published adult second-class prices for 2026 give you the shape of it:

Pass Price
4 days within 1 month €293
5 days within 1 month €335
7 days within 1 month €396
10 days within 2 months €472
15 days within 2 months €583
15 days continuous €487
1 month continuous €703
3 months continuous €1,054

First class adds roughly 30%. Under 28s get about 25% off, travellers 60 and over about 10%, and children aged 4–11 travel free with an adult, up to two children per adult — which quietly makes a family trip one of the strongest cases for a pass.

Note what the flexi prices imply: 5 days for €335 is €67 a travel day. That is your break-even number, and everything below turns on it.

The reservation trap, which is the whole game

This is where the “unlimited travel” promise meets reality. On many fast and long-distance trains a reservation is compulsory and paid separately, even with a valid pass:

  • France — every TGV, TGV Lyria and most Intercités require one, generally €10–30. TGVs sell a limited number of €10 passholder places; once those are gone on a given train, it’s €20.
  • Italy — Frecciarossa, Frecciargento and Italo all require reservations, roughly €10–13.
  • Spain — AVE, Alvia, Avant and most long-distance Renfe services require one.

So budget €10 or more per fast train, on top of the pass. On a multi-country trip that is not a rounding error; it is often the difference between the pass winning and losing.

The flip side, in fairness: a reservation fee is almost always far cheaper than a full-price walk-up ticket. The pass is not a scam. It is just not the all-you-can-ride product the marketing implies.

A valid pass does not guarantee you a seat

This is the part that ruins itineraries, and almost nobody plans for it.

Each TGV sells only a fixed quota of passholder places — around fifty. In peak season those sell out weeks in advance. Your pass is valid; the train is full for passholders; and you are choosing between paying for a normal ticket, taking a slower route, or travelling on a different day.

The practical consequence: if your trip has fixed dates and must-make connections, reserve the moment you can. A pass suits a flexible traveller. It punishes a tight schedule — which is the opposite of how it is usually sold.

There is also a Eurail Plus Pass, new for 2026 and still labelled beta, which bundles reservation fees into the pass price and includes a few “high-demand credits” for things like Eurostar and French high-speed seats. If it survives beta, it addresses exactly this complaint — worth checking whether it applies to your route before buying the standard version.

When a pass genuinely wins

A pass is usually the right call when:

  • You’re covering long distances across several countries — the more border-crossing travel days, the better the arithmetic.
  • Your plans are loose. The real product you are buying is the freedom to decide on the morning.
  • You’re travelling with children 4–11, who ride free with an adult.
  • You’re under 28, where the discount moves the break-even substantially.
  • You’d otherwise be buying short-notice tickets, which is where European rail gets genuinely expensive.

Point-to-point tickets usually win when:

  • Your route is fixed and known — advance fares released months ahead in France, Spain, Italy and Germany are frequently cheaper than a pass day plus its reservation.
  • You’re staying mostly in one country, where domestic passes or regional tickets are often better value.
  • Your trip is a few long hops with several nights in between. Two travel days do not justify a pass.
  • You’ll be on regional trains, which rarely need reservations and are cheap anyway.

The honest test: write down the journeys you will actually take, price each as an advance ticket, then compare that against the pass price plus €10–20 per fast train. If the pass doesn’t win clearly, it isn’t winning — the flexibility premium is real but it is not usually worth €100.

How to decide, in order

  1. List your actual train journeys with dates. Not the ones you might take.
  2. Price each as an advance point-to-point fare on the national operator’s own site.
  3. Price the pass, then add a realistic reservation cost for every fast train on the list.
  4. Check the passholder quota situation for any peak-season high-speed leg before you commit.
  5. Only then decide — and if you buy a pass, reserve your fixed-date legs immediately.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between Eurail and Interrail in 2026?

As of 9 September 2026 there is none: the two ranges merged into a single product called Interrail, open to everyone. Before that, eligibility depended on residence rather than nationality, with Interrail for people who had lived in Europe for at least six months. Because the change is very recent, confirm current terms on the official site.

Do I still pay for seat reservations with a rail pass?

Yes, on many fast and long-distance trains, and it is compulsory. French TGVs are generally 10-30 euro, with a limited number of 10-euro passholder places before the price rises to 20; Italy's Frecciarossa, Frecciargento and Italo run about 10-13 euro; Spain's AVE and Alvia also require one. Budget 10 euro or more per fast train on top of the pass.

Does a valid rail pass guarantee me a seat?

No. Each high-speed train sells only a fixed quota of passholder places, around fifty on a TGV, and in peak season these sell out weeks ahead. A valid pass on a full train means paying for a normal ticket, rerouting, or travelling another day.

Is a Europe rail pass cheaper than buying tickets?

It depends on how many long cross-border journeys you make and how far ahead you book. Advance point-to-point fares often beat a pass on a fixed known route, while a pass wins on multi-country trips, loose plans, short-notice travel, and for under-28s and families with children aged 4 to 11, who travel free with an adult.

Should I choose a flexi or continuous rail pass?

Flexi if you have several nights in each place, since you only spend a travel day when you move. Continuous if you are moving most days: 15 days continuous at 487 euro undercuts 15 flexi days at 583 euro.
